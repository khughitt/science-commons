## Summary

Estrogen, progesterone, and androgens are primary determinants of immune system state across two timescales: within the menstrual cycle (infradian, ~28-day oscillation) and across reproductive-stage transitions (puberty, pregnancy, menopause). Both timescales produce measurable, mechanistically grounded changes in innate and adaptive immunity — TLR expression, cytokine profiles, Th1/Th2/Th17/Treg balance, B-cell tolerance, autophagy flux, mast-cell reactivity, and microglial activation. These changes are not incidental to reproduction; they are the mechanism by which the hormonal cycle exerts systemic, non-reproductive physiological control. The strongest downstream consequences appear at transition points: pregnancy-induced remission of Th1-mediated autoimmune disease, postpartum rebound, perimenopausal pro-inflammatory shift, and postmenopausal metabolic-neuroinflammatory reorganization. Immune biomarkers sampled without recording cycle phase or reproductive stage carry a structural confound that is systematic, large in magnitude, and currently under-corrected in clinical and epidemiological research.

## Current State of Knowledge

### Estrogen biphasic immunomodulation

The dose-response profile of estrogen on immunity is established across multiple independent lines of evidence: in vitro receptor pharmacology (ERα vs ERβ signaling on immune cells), ex vivo cytokine assays at different cycle phases, humanized mouse models with estradiol supplementation or castration, and clinical disease patterns at pregnancy (high estrogen) versus menopause (low estrogen). The biphasic response — pro-inflammatory at low dose, anti-inflammatory at high dose — is reproduced across Th1/Th2 balance, NK cytotoxicity, dendritic-cell MHC II expression, and B-cell apoptosis thresholds. The concentration dependence means that cycle-phase effects on immunity are fundamentally non-linear: the follicular and luteal phases produce different, not merely graded, immune phenotypes.

### Th1/Th2/Th17/Treg shifts

The dominant model is that estrogen favors Th2 and Treg at high concentrations while testosterone and low estrogen favor Th1 and Th17. The Th17/Treg ratio rises at menopause and in flare states. Progesterone, undercharacterized relative to estrogen, drives secretory-phase autophagy, promotes decidual immune tolerance (uNK residence, M2 macrophage polarization), and is broadly immunosuppressive in the luteal phase. The clinical downstream is: Th1-mediated diseases (MS, RA, T1DM) remit in high-estrogen / high-progesterone states (pregnancy, luteal phase) and flare in low-estrogen states (postpartum, menopause); Th2-mediated diseases (SLE, allergic) can worsen when Th2 is amplified. This cross-disease pattern is among the strongest evidence for hormonal systemic immune control.

### Pregnancy suppression and postpartum/menopause flares

Pregnancy is a natural experiment in extreme hormonal state. MS relapse reduction of 50–70% in the third trimester, followed by rebound within 3 months postpartum, demonstrates time-locked immunosuppression and restoration that cannot be explained by behavior or other confounders alone. RA similarly remits in pregnancy and flares postpartum and at menopause. The postpartum rebound is a causal signature: the immunosuppressive constraint was real, not a selection artifact.

### X-chromosome dosage

The female XX genotype creates a structural substrate for amplified immune responses: approximately double the dose of X-linked immune regulators, X-linked miRNAs (~10% of the human miRNome on X versus 2 on Y), and the XIST autoantigenic complex. These genetic effects compound with hormonal effects rather than being independent of them — XIST autoimmunity appears to be amplified under the pro-inflammatory, low-estrogen conditions of menopause.
