## Key Findings

### Hematopoietic hierarchy and mutation accumulation

- HSCs in adult humans number approximately 4.4–21.5 × 10⁴ active cells at any moment; their longevity (self-renewal over the organism's lifespan) and low division rate make them the principal reservoir for somatic mutation accumulation.
- CHIP — defined as clones constituting >2% of blood cells — is present in >10% of people above age 60, driven predominantly by DNMT3A, TET2, and ASXL1 mutations (epigenetic regulators). CHIP confers significantly elevated risk of overt leukemia.
- Preleukemic HSCs retain multilineage reconstitution capacity and produce non-leukemic progeny, distinguishing them from leukemia stem cells (LSCs).

### The stepwise model and mutation order

- Two categories of mutations operate in sequence: (1) "landscaping" mutations in epigenetic regulators establish preleukemic clones at the HSC level; (2) "transforming/signaling" mutations (e.g., FLT3-ITD, NPM1, BCR-ABL1) occur later at the progenitor level and complete malignant transformation.
- The order of mutation acquisition determines disease kinetics: in MPNs carrying TET2 and JAK2 mutations, landscaping (TET2) first produces older patients at diagnosis (longer latency), while signaling (JAK2) first leads to younger patients at diagnosis (shorter time to full transformation). This was elegantly demonstrated by Ortmann et al.
- LSCs in AML acquire self-renewal by the final transforming hit; they typically have a more differentiated progenitor immunophenotype (CD34⁺CD38⁻) despite self-renewal capacity.

### AML: paradigmatic HSC-source disease

- First somatic mutations in AML arise in landscaping genes (DNMT3A, TET2, IDH1/2, cohesin complex members) at the HSC level; these can be detected years before diagnosis.
- Late genetic events (FLT3-ITD, NPM1) arise in progenitors and are relatively unstable between diagnosis and relapse.
- Preleukemic HSCs survive standard induction chemotherapy, persist during remission (evidenced by detection in complete remission patients), and serve as founders of relapse clones. DNMT3A/TET2/ASXL1/SRSF2 persistence during first remission predicts adverse clinical outcomes.
- Two relapse models are discussed: (A) incomplete LSC eradication by chemotherapy reconstituting the dominant clone; (B) complete LSC eradication by chemotherapy, but genotoxic damage to surviving preleukemic HSCs generates new LSC clones.

### CML: a single-hit HSC disease with different epigenetic landscape requirements

- The BCR-ABL1 translocation at the HSC level is necessary (and largely sufficient) to initiate CML — but BCR-ABL1 alone does not confer self-renewal; natural HSC self-renewal is required for CML development.
- Landscaping mutations are present in only 15–20% of CML patients (contrast with AML); the Philadelphia translocation itself acts as a "landscaping" step, promoting further genomic instability.
- Quiescent LSCs (CD45RAˉcˉkitˉCD26⁺ fraction) are resistant to TKI therapy (BCR-ABL1 kinase-independent survival), making curative eradication impossible by conventional TKI alone.
- Clonal diversity of BCR-ABL1⁺ HSCs/LSCs is substantial; single-cell analyses reveal functional heterogeneity in response to TKIs.

### ALL: variable HSC involvement by subtype

- ALL cellular origin is heterogeneous: HSCs are most clearly implicated in BCR-ABLp210⁺ ALL (same as CML source) and in KMT2A-rearranged infant ALL (where in utero prenatal mutations occur at or near the HSC level).
- ETV6-RUNX1 (the most common translocation, ~25% of pediatric B-ALL): acts on HSC level in mouse models (ETV6-RUNX1⁺ HSCs sustain normal hematopoiesis quiescently without growth advantage); the final transforming hits occur at B-lineage committed progenitors. HSCs serve as a "silent reservoir" of the preleukemic clone.
- BCR-ABLp190⁺ ALL: fusion arises in B-cell committed progenitors (CD34⁺CD38ˉCD19ˉ), not at the multipotent HSC level; distinguishes this from BCR-ABLp210⁺.
- Consensus on ALL cellular origin is still elusive; B cell stem-like hierarchy is disturbed in pediatric ALL, complicating immunophenotypic inference of cell-of-origin.

### CLL: HSC-driven B-lineage bias initiating monoclonal B lymphocytosis

- CLL is preceded by monoclonal B lymphocytosis (MBL); HSCs in CLL patients show biased differentiation toward B-lineage even before CLL clone is detectable.
- Key driver mutations (NOTCH1, SF3B1, TP53, XPO1, MYD88, FBXW7) are detectable in CD34⁺ hematopoietic progenitors in the majority of CLL patients.
- CLL-HSCs transplanted into immunodeficient mice regenerate their own population and give rise to CLL-like CD19⁺ B cells (not normal B-cell diversity), supporting HSCs as cell-of-origin in CLL.
- The model: HSC-driven clonal hematopoiesis with B-lineage bias → MBL → CLL through continued somatic evolution under antigenic and inflammatory stimuli.

### Mature cell malignancies

- In hairy cell leukemia (HCL), BRAFV600E mutation is present in HSCs (CD34⁺CD38ˉ) as well as lymphoid progenitors; HSC-level expression drives lethal disease in mouse models whereas B-cell-restricted expression does not — confirming HSC-level origin.
- In multiple myeloma (MM), TP53 and RUNX1 mutations found in secondary AML/MDS post-HCT were already present in HSPCs years before transplantation, implicating preleukemic HSCs in therapy-related myeloid neoplasm development.
- B-cell lymphomas: CREBBP deletion at HSC level causes aggressive lymphoma in mice; MALT1 overexpression in HSCs initiates MALT lymphoma-like disease. Evidence for T-cell lymphomas links DNMT3A/TET2 CHIP mutations to clonal hematopoiesis preceding lymphomagenesis.
- The discrimination between direct HSC-derived versus CHIP-mediated-inflammatory indirect contributions to lymphoma remains unresolved.

### Therapeutic and clinical implications

- Preleukemic HSCs are resistant to standard chemotherapy, survive remission, and can be re-infused in autologous HCT — contributing to relapse and therapy-related neoplasms.
- MRD detection using early landscaping mutations (DNMT3A) risks identifying preleukemic rather than leukemic clones; late mutations (absent in preleukemic HSCs) are more specific but unstable during therapy.
- Eradicating preleukemic HSCs requires strategies beyond conventional chemotherapy: epigenetic agents targeting DNMT3A/TET2 mutations, immune-based approaches (though neoantigen burden is low in preleukemic HSCs), or anti-inflammatory interventions targeting chronic inflammation that drives clonal expansion.
- Pre-transplant CHIP screening of donors for allogenic HCT is proposed as a strategy to reduce donor-derived leukemia risk.

## Limitations

- As a narrative review, it does not perform quantitative synthesis or meta-analysis; selection of cited studies may reflect authors' interpretive priors.
- The evidence for HSC involvement is strongest for AML and CML; for ALL, CLL, and lymphomas the mechanistic chain is less complete and often rests on mouse models that may not faithfully recapitulate human disease hierarchy.
- The distinction between preleukemic HSC (cell-of-origin) and LSC (leukemia-propagating cell) is carefully defined in Box 1 but the ambiguity noted by the authors themselves — that many studies use these terms interchangeably — means cited evidence may conflate the two populations.
- The HSC pool is intrinsically heterogeneous (the review acknowledges single-cell analysis as the necessary resolution level), and bulk CD34⁺CD38ˉ phenotyping may include MPPs; this complicates interpretation of transplantation and sequencing studies.
- The review does not address non-hematopoietic malignancies or inter-tissue generalizations; its scope is intentionally confined to blood.
- Therapeutic implications (epigenetic reprogramming of preleukemic HSCs, anti-inflammatory prevention, donor CHIP screening) are prospective and largely untested in clinical settings.
