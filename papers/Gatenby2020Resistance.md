## Key Findings

- **Molecular resistance is only step one.** A resistant phenotype matters clinically only if it expands into a large enough population to drive progression.
- **MTD causes competitive release when cure fails.** Killing sensitive cells can remove the ecological suppression that previously constrained resistant cells.
- **Adaptive therapy preserves sensitive competitors.** Treatment is cycled or modulated to keep resistant cells suppressed by drug-sensitive cells while maintaining acceptable tumor burden.
- **Double-bind therapy exploits collateral sensitivity.** A first drug is used to select resistance that creates vulnerability to a second drug, making timing and sequence central.
- **Extinction therapy targets small fragmented residual populations.** The first strike reduces tumor burden, then second strikes are sequenced before relapse to prevent ecological recovery and evolutionary rescue.
- **Dynamic measurement is a bottleneck.** Evolution-based therapy requires repeated measures of tumor burden, phenotypes, and ecosystem state, not only one-time biomarkers.

## Limitations

- The paper is primarily conceptual and review-based.
- Clinical evidence for adaptive therapy was early at publication time, with most support from small or nonrandomized studies.
- It emphasizes competition between sensitive and resistant cells but only partially covers plasticity, immune state, and multi-lesion heterogeneity.
- Translational success depends on monitoring and model calibration infrastructure that remains underdeveloped.
