## Key Findings

**Mutational burden**
- Normal sun-exposed skin has the highest somatic mutation burden of any surveyed normal tissue: 2–6 mutations per megabase per cell (landmark eyelid-skin TDS study, n = 4 donors, 234 samples).
- Median burden in WES of sun-exposed vs. non-exposed skin: 10.4 Mb vs. 0.25 Mb, respectively.
- Each skin cell carries 402–14,029 base substitutions plus 7–71 indels (single-cell WGS, n = 21).
- Mutation burden accumulates exponentially with age, further modulated by skin site, sun-damage history, and phototype.

**Driver genes under positive selection**
- Consistently positively selected: NOTCH1, NOTCH2, FAT1, TP53 (across multiple studies).
- Also reported: PPM1D, KMT2D, ASXL1, RBM10, NOTCH3.
- Site-specificity: TP53 preferentially selected in head; FAT1 preferentially selected in leg.
- Normal skin clones average ~0.27 driver point mutations per cell vs. ~2.7 per cSCC tumour; some normal-skin clones harbour 2–3 drivers.
- Driver gene profiles resemble cSCC more than BCC or melanoma; PTCH1 (BCC driver) is rarely mutated in normal skin.
- ~140 cancer driver mutations per cm² in positively selected normal-skin cells (18–32% of cells); variant allele frequencies typically <10%, indicating small clone sizes.

**Dual roles of driver clones: cancer-promoting vs. cancer-protective**
- Cancer-promoting model: positive selection tracks with known cSCC risk factors (age, UV, site); certain drivers (TP53, NOTCH2, KMT2D) are further enriched in cSCC vs. normal skin.
- Cancer-protective model: NOTCH genes are sometimes more frequent in normal tissue than in the corresponding cancer; peak melanoma age is delayed in NOTCH1-mutant patients; mouse models show Notch1-mutant normal cells can outcompete and extrude early wild-type neoplasms, restoring tissue homeostasis.
- CDKN2A point mutations are frequent in cSCC but rare in normal skin, implying CDKN2A inactivation is cancer-specific rather than a normal-tissue precursor event.
- Mutant-clone expansion is strong only during the initial exponential phase; neutral drift subsequently dominates — consistent with clones plateauing at small size without transforming.

**Canonical hotspot mutations**
- Present but rare in normal skin: FGFR3 K652E/Y375C, KRAS G12V/G12D, HRAS G12D/G13D, NRAS Q61R, PIK3CA H1047Y, and variants of BRAF, PIK3CA, FGFR3.
- BRAF codon-600 and TERT promoter mutations not observed in normal melanocytes.
- Druggable hotspots in normal skin carry therapeutic-relevance implications but their clinical significance remains unclear.

**Mutational signatures**
- Dominant signature: UV (SBS7a + SBS7b) with strand bias; exponentially increases with age.
- Secondary signatures: aging (SBS1 + SBS5); C>T in CTT contexts (resembles SBS17a, unknown aetiology; prevalent in younger donors <63 years); C>A without obvious context (8-oxoguanine oxidative damage, also seen in cSCC).
- Decline in transcription-coupled nucleotide excision repair (TC-NER) fidelity with age may explain the exponential UV-signature rise.
- APOBEC (SBS2/13) and reactive oxygen species (SBS18) signatures (present in cutaneous melanoma) were not identified as major normal-skin signatures in reviewed studies.

**Copy number alterations (CNAs)**
- CNAs are rare in normal skin relative to cancers.
- Most common CNA: loss of the 9q region encompassing NOTCH1; identified in 11.5% (27/234), 4.3% (2/46), and 5.1% (2/39) of samples across three studies.
- Most NOTCH1 CNAs co-occur with NOTCH1 point mutations, suggesting biallelic inactivation within single normal-skin clones.
- CNAs may represent a later event in skin carcinogenesis relative to point mutations.

**Methodological considerations**
- Multi-regional sampling essential: absence of a clonal unit means mosaic clones are distributed across tissue without structural boundaries (unlike colon crypts or endometrial glands).
- TDS offers highest read depth at lower cost but cannot capture off-panel drivers or genome-wide mutational signatures.
- WGS/WES are more comprehensive but require larger sample amounts and deeper coverage to detect small clones.
- Contamination by dermal fibroblasts and leukocytes must be avoided when studying keratinocytes; hair-follicle vs. interfollicular epidermis have distinct mutational landscapes.

## Limitations

- Narrative review: no systematic search protocol or PRISMA flowchart; selection of primary studies reflects authors' prior work prominently (ref 33 = Kim YS et al. 2023 is from the same group).
- Most primary studies reviewed used TDS of bulk keratinocytes; single-cell and melanocyte-specific data remain sparse.
- Studies predominantly Caucasian or Korean donors; racial/ethnic generalisability of UV-signature findings is acknowledged but not resolved.
- The cancer-protective vs. cancer-promoting dichotomy is largely built from esophageal Notch models; direct experimental skin evidence is limited.
- CDKN2A as a progression gatekeeper is inferred from frequency differences between normal and cancerous tissue; longitudinal tracking of individual clones is absent.
- CNAs remain poorly characterised due to sensitivity limitations of current methods for small clones.
- No longitudinal cohort data: the review cannot directly address whether a given clone observed in normal skin will progress.
