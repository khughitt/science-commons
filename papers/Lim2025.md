## One-Sentence Summary

A pan-disease analysis across 32 diseases identifies three generalised proteostasis perturbation states that distinguish cancer, neurodegenerative, and other disease types, reveals stage-dependent temporal dynamics of proteostasis disruption, and shows that smoking primes the proteostasis network in directions concordant with smoking-associated disease risk.

## Key Findings

1. **Proteostasis proteins are broadly and strongly disease-associated.** Hypergeometric over-representation analysis shows proteostasis network (PN) proteins are significantly enriched in the top-500 disease-associated gene sets across all 7 disease groups studied, with enrichment comparable to — and in some groups exceeding — kinases (a major drug-target class). Ion channels, used as a negative control, are enriched only in neurodegenerative and cardiovascular disease groups as expected.

2. **Proteostasis fraction of disease gene sets is 20–36%, highest in cancer and neurodegeneration.** Across 32 diseases, proteostasis proteins constitute 25–36% of disease gene sets in cancer and 30–35% in neurodegenerative diseases (Fig 2). Autophagy-lysosome pathway (ALP) and proteostasis regulation are consistently over-represented across all diseases; ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS) proteins are preferentially enriched in cancer and neurodegenerative diseases but not other disease groups; extracellular proteostasis proteins are over-represented in all groups except cancer.

3. **Three generalised proteostasis perturbation states discriminate disease types.** Based on ALP, UPS, and extracellular proteostasis (ER) involvement:
   - **State 1 (ALP + UPS- + ER-):** Cancer — significant UPS perturbation, limited extracellular proteostasis.
   - **State 2 (ALP + UPS + ER+):** Neurodegenerative diseases — extensive perturbation of both UPS and extracellular proteostasis.
   - **State 3 (ALP + UPS- + ER+):** Autoimmune, endocrine, cardiovascular, reproductive, and respiratory diseases — distinctive extracellular proteostasis deregulation with limited UPS involvement.

4. **Unsupervised clustering by proteostasis signatures recovers disease type with one notable exception.** Hierarchical clustering of the 32 diseases by Jaccard similarity of their proteostasis gene sets produces 4 clusters that largely follow disease type. Exception: pancreatic cancer and kidney cancer cluster with autoimmune diseases rather than other cancers, consistent with reported bidirectional associations between cancer and autoimmune disorders.

5. **Proteostasis perturbations are early in cancer but progressive in neurodegeneration.** Differential expression analysis across disease stages (3 cancers and 3 neurodegenerative diseases with publicly available staging data) shows that ALP and UPS genes are perturbed predominantly at early stages in cancers (lung SCLC, kidney, pancreatic) but accumulate progressively in later stages of neurodegenerative diseases (AD Braak 1–6, PD Braak 3–6, HTT Vonsattel 3–4). This is conserved across all 6 diseases examined.

6. **Mid-stage AD proteostasis proteins are most central in the PN interaction network.** Contrary to the initial hypothesis that early-stage proteins would be most central (as upstream regulators), degree and betweenness centrality analysis of the AD proteostasis subnetwork shows that Braak 2/3 (mid-stage) proteins have the highest network centrality. This suggests mid-stage disruptions may seed downstream proteostasis collapse in later AD stages.

7. **Smoking proteostasis perturbations recapitulate disease-risk directionality.** Comparing smoking-induced DEGs (GSE22047, GSE108134; smokers vs non-smokers) against disease gene sets for 8 diseases (4 at-risk: COPD, lung cancer, breast cancer, coronary heart disease; 4 reduced-risk: ulcerative colitis, endometrial cancer, endometriosis, PD): Jaccard similarity of perturbed proteostasis proteins is higher between smokers and at-risk diseases, and directional similarity (up/down concordance) is positive for at-risk diseases and negative for reduced-risk diseases. This proteostasis-based similarity is more disease-discriminating than smoking-impacted kinases, transcription factors, or a random DEG set of equal size.
   - CCL2 is upregulated in both smokers and COPD patients (an at-risk disease example).
   - UCHL1 is upregulated in smokers but downregulated in PD patients — directionally opposite, consistent with PD being a reduced-risk disease for smokers and with UCHL1 downregulation contributing to Lewy body pathology.

## Limitations

- **Disease gene sets are multi-omics composites (PandaOmics), not pure transcriptomic.** They aggregate literature, interactome, OMIM, and expression signals via a neural network — making the "molecular" interpretation less clean than direct DEG lists. For this project, this means they cannot cleanly serve as Track A molecular truth for h01.
- **Top-500 gene cutoff is arbitrary.** Disease gene sets defined by a fixed-rank cutoff (rather than significance threshold) means the proportion of proteostasis proteins is partially a function of the gene list depth, not just disease biology.
- **Pancreatic/kidney cancer cluster with autoimmune — causality unknown.** The paper attributes this to the known bidirectional cancer–autoimmune relationship, but the proteostasis mechanism underlying this clustering is not investigated.
- **Staging analyses cover only 6 diseases with publicly available staging transcriptomic data.** The generalizability of the early-in-cancer vs progressive-in-neurodegeneration pattern is asserted broadly but only demonstrated in a subset.
- **Smoking analysis is correlational.** The proteostasis similarity between smokers and at-risk diseases is presented as evidence of a "priming" mechanism, but no causal demonstration is provided. Confounders (tissue type, age, sex) in the smoking vs non-smoking transcriptomic datasets are not discussed.
- **Proteostasis network scope depends on the Proteostasis Consortium's curation.** The completeness and accuracy of the PN component list is not independently assessed within the paper.
