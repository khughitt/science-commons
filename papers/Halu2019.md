## Key Findings

1. **Genotype and phenotype layers are largely non-overlapping.** Only 139 disease-disease edges are shared across both layers (significantly more than the random expectation of 31.80 ± 5.3, z-score = 20.8). Yet the community structure is strongly layer-specific: the average Jaccard overlap of communities across layers is J = 0.00286 ± 0.000289, indistinguishable from degree-preserving randomized networks (z-score = 0.882). This means diseases group differently according to genetic vs. symptomatic criteria — a direct quantification of the genotype-phenotype disconnect.

2. **Overlapping edges (shared genes AND shared symptoms) are biologically enriched.** The 139 overlapping edges connect well-characterized disease pairs: e.g., MASA syndrome and X-linked hydrocephalus (same mutation), cerebral amyloid angiopathy and Alzheimer disease (same gene, different severity), Roberts syndrome and SC Phocomelia (same gene, subtypes), cystic fibrosis and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (shared molecular mechanism newly recognized). These "overlap hubs" are multi-system diseases with common genetic factors affecting multiple organs.

3. **Multiplex communities are biologically cohesive at both levels simultaneously.** Of 29 communities with ≥10 diseases, 26/29 show significantly high comorbidity (RR), 20/29 for gene overlap (Jaccard), 23/29 for GO:BP similarity, 27/29 for phenotype semantic similarity (MimMiner). Critically, 29/29 have significantly high similarity by at least one of the four measures, and 14/29 are significantly high by all four.

4. **Multiplex communities outperform single-layer communities on both axes.** Multiplex communities perform better than genotype-layer communities on phenotypic similarity measures, and better than phenotype-layer communities on molecular similarity measures. Multiplex communities score slightly better than single-layer communities on all four measures overall, suggesting the multiplex is "greater than the sum of its parts" — each layer compensates for the other's blind spots.

5. **"Bridge diseases" (215 of 779) connect genotypic and phenotypic communities.** These diseases are assigned to the same multiplex community across both layers while belonging to different single-layer communities. They represent cross-layer connectors: e.g., age-related macular degeneration and acute lymphocytic leukemia are in the same multiplex community (Community 15) despite no shared phenotype edges and only two shared genotype-connected diseases, linked by the ocular comorbidity of acute leukemia.

6. **Novel disease associations uncovered.** Within multiplex communities, pairs with low literature co-occurrence but high biological similarity are identified as putatively novel. Example: fibular hypoplasia and complex brachydactyly are grouped with acromesomelic dysplasia types by shared GDF5 mutations, despite not co-occurring in the literature. Similarly, synpolydactyly and acrocapitofemoral dysplasia (no shared genes) are grouped by high comorbidity and GO:BP similarity, suggesting underlying shared mechanisms for these digit dysplasias.

7. **Disease Community 8 (rare congenital heart defects + skeletal anomalies) is a detailed case study.** DiGeorge syndrome, velocardiofacial syndrome, Tetralogy of Fallot, ventricular septal defect, and related conditions cluster together. GO:BP validation confirms outflow tract morphogenesis (GO:0003151) and heart morphogenesis (GO:0003007) as shared biological processes: S_GOBP = 0.687–0.747 (z-scores 2.57–2.87) for DiGeorge vs. ventricular septal defect comparisons. The community bridges skeletal anomalies (Hajdu-Cheney syndrome) to cardiac conditions via cardiovascular manifestations of the bone disorder.

8. **Monogenic disorders predispose to complex diseases in same multiplex communities.** Community 12 groups autism spectrum disorder with Joubert Syndrome, Senior-Loken Syndrome, and other ciliopathies (NPHP1 deletions). Community 16 groups rare syndromes (Denys-Drash, Frasier) with breast/basal cell carcinoma and other cancers. Community 22 groups Wolfram syndrome with type 2 diabetes mellitus and diabetic ketoacidosis. Community 21 groups DiGeorge syndrome with schizophrenia and alcohol dependence.

9. **GWAS-based genotype layer yields concordant communities** (SI Section 12), validating that the OMIM-based findings are not artifact of database bias.

10. **Community detection outperforms random baselines.** Intra-community publication co-occurrence is higher than inter-community co-occurrence for nearly all communities (SI Section 11). Multiplex communities contain more novel Disease-Connect-evidenced edges than either single-layer community partition.

## Limitations

- **Unweighted edges.** A single shared gene or symptom is sufficient to create an edge, regardless of how many are shared. This risks over-inclusion of edges due to promiscuous/common symptoms (e.g., "fever") or pleiotropic genes. Weighted layers would reduce noise.
- **OMIM bias toward Mendelian/rare disease.** OMIM over-represents rare Mendelian disorders; common complex diseases have fewer curated gene entries, leading to sparse genotype-layer coverage. The phenotype layer (HPO) has better coverage of complex diseases.
- **Disease Ontology integration loss.** Integrating OMIM and HPO via Disease Ontology collapsed 3919 shared diseases to 779 (after removing isolated nodes), losing ~80% of diseases. The resulting network may not represent common diseases well.
- **No edge weights or direction.** The multiplex framework is symmetric; disease A predisposing to disease B is not distinguished from the reverse. Bridge disease analysis is undirected.
- **Static snapshot.** Network built from databases as they stood in ~2017-2018. Disease-gene and disease-symptom knowledge is rapidly evolving; many associations (especially for rare diseases) remain incomplete.
- **Multiplex community assignment ambiguity.** A disease can belong to the same community in both layers OR different communities. The 215 "bridge diseases" spanning two communities add interpretive complexity — the authors acknowledge this as a feature, but it complicates downstream applications.
- **Community detection algorithm choice.** Multiplex Infomap is used throughout; the authors note (SI Section 3) that results depend on the relax rate parameter (set to 0.45 throughout) and that other methods could yield different partitions. No sensitivity analysis across multiple algorithms is presented in the main text.
- **Validation circularity risk.** Literature co-occurrence (PubAtlas) is used to distinguish "known" from "novel" associations within communities, but literature co-occurrence is itself subject to publication gravity — heavily-studied disease pairs will have high co-occurrence regardless of true biological relationship. This is the same confound identified in our project's publication-gravity memory note.
