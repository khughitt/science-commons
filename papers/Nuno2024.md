## Key Findings

**1. ~40% of relapsed AMLs lack driver mutation evolution.**
In the meta-analysis of 216 patients, 93 (43%) fell in the "stable" clonal bin (no gain or loss of driver mutations at relapse). Patients with stable clonality had significantly shorter time-to-relapse (HR = 1.56, p = 0.007 after age/sex correction) than those gaining or losing mutations, suggesting genomically stable cases represent a more intrinsically aggressive disease state rather than a less-evolved one.

**2. A shared relapse chromatin accessibility signature in mutationally stable AML.**
Across all 26 patients, stable-clonality cases showed the lowest diagnosis-to-relapse Pearson chromatin similarity (mean DX-REL correlation: Stable 0.914; Gain 0.929; Loss 0.950; Gain+Loss 0.802), indicating they undergo substantial epigenetic evolution despite genomic stability. Differential accessibility analysis of 26 stable samples identified 2,373 peaks enriched and 6,145 peaks depleted at relapse (out of 115,551). The enriched signature was characterized by:
   - Increased accessibility at histone genes, epigenetic regulators, and surface marker CD69
   - GSEA enrichment for: MYC targets V1 and V2, oxidative phosphorylation, G2M checkpoint, DNA repair, mTORC1 signaling, unfolded protein response, reactive oxygen species, heme metabolism
   - Depletion of: KRAS signaling DN, epithelial-mesenchymal transition, adipogenesis, apoptosis
   - Transcription factor analysis: depletion of AP1 family motifs (FOS, JUN) and CEBPA/SPI1 (myeloid differentiation regulators); enrichment of FOXO family, GATA family, and E2F — implicating progenitor re-activation and cell cycle progression programs
   - Chromosomal topology: chromosome 6 contained a ~100 kb region of widespread increased accessibility spanning histone and cell-cycle genes at relapse, suggesting regional co-regulation

Notably, this signature was specific to stable samples: the differentially accessible genes in stable relapsed AMLs were NOT significantly differentially accessible in non-stable cases — confirming stable AMLs undergo a qualitatively distinct epigenetic evolution.

**3. Epigenetic changes at relapse occur primarily in non-LSC compartments.**
LSC-enriched populations (CD34+CD38−CD99+TIM3+) showed significantly less global chromatin change at relapse than non-LSC mature blasts (p = 0.02 paired comparison). Key observations:
   - Almost half of differentially accessible features in LSCs were also differentially accessible in non-LSCs; fewer than 5% of features differentially accessible in non-LSCs were also differentially accessible in LSCs
   - This asymmetry suggests directional inheritance: LSC chromatin changes are propagated to non-LSC progeny, but non-LSC changes are not fed back to LSCs
   - The bulk AML stable relapse signature strongly resembled the LSC-vs.-non-LSC signature at diagnosis, suggesting bulk relapse epigenetic evolution reflects a shift toward more LSC-like chromatin state in the non-LSC compartment
   - LSC-specific GSEA at relapse: enrichment for inflammatory signaling (IFN-γ response, IL-2/STAT5, TNF-α/NF-κB), consistent with chemotherapy-induced inflammatory program acquisition

**4. Pre-existing relapse-fated epigenetic subpopulations at diagnosis.**
ScATAC-seq of 4 patients (SU142, SU360, SU484, SU892) revealed substantial intra-patient epigenetic heterogeneity at diagnosis. Scoring cells with the bulk relapse signature showed that in patients SU360, SU484, and SU892, subpopulations present at diagnosis harbored the relapse chromatin signature at levels comparable to relapse cells. In SU360, diagnosis clusters 7, 8, and 9 (representing 24% of AML) grouped with relapse clusters. In SU892, diagnosis clusters 1 and 2 (14% of AML) were most similar to relapse cells. SU142 showed minimal epigenetic change between diagnosis and relapse (consistent with MLL-rearrangement and minimal mutation change). These results indicate intercellular selection — therapy selecting pre-existing epigenetically primed subclones — rather than solely intracellular reprogramming.

**5. Mitochondrial clonal tracing demonstrates convergent epigenetic evolution.**
In mutationally stable AMLs (SU142, SU360, SU892), mtscATAC-seq identified 2–10 mitoclones per patient. Key findings:
   - Mitoclone size distributions were stable between diagnosis and relapse (no dominant clonal outgrowth in stable samples), confirming true genomic stability
   - At relapse, mitoclones became epigenetically more similar to each other than at diagnosis (increased inter-clone Pearson chromatin similarity; statistically significant in SU892 p = 0.0041, SU142 p = 0.0041)
   - Distinct mitoclones from SU360 (mitoclone 1 and 2) showed similar patterns of accessibility change at relapse despite distinct evolutionary origins — direct evidence of convergent intracellular epigenetic evolution
   - Scoring of mitoclones with the relapse signature showed all relapse mitoclones displayed higher relapse scores than diagnosis mitoclones (Figure 5 supplement 2)
   - In the genetically non-stable case SU484: 12 mitoclones identified; mitoclone 2 (0.2% at diagnosis) expanded to 36% at relapse, associated with FLT3-ITD gain; this mitoclone was progenitor-like at diagnosis and acquired further epigenetic divergence at relapse alongside expansion

**6. Global epigenetic dedifferentiation at relapse.**
Projection of all AML ATAC samples onto a healthy hematopoietic scATAC reference showed that AMLs overall became less differentiated at relapse — mapping to more CMP/GMP/progenitor-like states rather than myeloid/monocyte-like states. Every sample that was myeloid/monocyte-like at diagnosis became less differentiated at relapse; progenitor-like samples generally remained within the progenitor compartment. Patient-specific shifts included: SU484 becoming substantially more immature (LMPP/CMP-like) at relapse; SU892 relapse cells mapping to dendritic/basophil and erythroid-like states (neomorphic epigenetic state).

## Limitations

- Bulk ATAC-seq cohort is n = 26, limiting statistical power for subgroup analyses (e.g., mutation-specific chromatin evolution); scATAC-seq and mtscATAC-seq cohorts are n = 4 each — results, especially convergence statistics, should be treated as proof-of-concept pending replication in larger cohorts.
- ATAC-seq measures chromatin accessibility (open chromatin), not histone modifications (H3K27me3, H3K4me3) or DNA methylation — the study cannot directly address bivalent chromatin as the mechanistic substrate of epigenetic memory (q017 remains unresolved by this paper).
- The LSC immunophenotype (CD34+CD38−CD99+TIM3+) is incompletely validated for the relapse setting: the authors acknowledge this but note consistent functional behavior of these cells. LSC enrichment purity is not quantified beyond FACS gating.
- The stable-sample chromatin signature is derived from a group averaging approach: individual patient-level epigenetic evolution is heterogeneous (as shown in the scATAC data), so the shared signature represents a common component, not a universal patient-level signature.
- Remission samples were not profiled — it is not possible to determine whether epigenetic evolution occurs during treatment, during remission, or at both stages. The model cannot distinguish "selected during chemotherapy" from "evolved during regrowth in remission."
- mtscATAC-seq heteroplasmy-based clonal tracing captures evolution over the timescale of mitochondrial variant accumulation; in stable AMLs that relapsed quickly (shorter time-to-relapse), fewer mitoclones may be defined, limiting resolution.
- Cytogenetics and specific mutation combinations were not systematically analyzed as predictors of whether a patient will show epigenetic vs. genetic evolution at relapse — the study does not provide a predictive model.
- No functional validation (e.g., epigenetic inhibitor experiments in relapsed AML models, or chromatin perturbation of the relapse signature) is presented; the causal contribution of the signature to relapse remains correlative.
