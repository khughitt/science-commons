## Key Findings

### Chronotype and cancer risk (observational)

The preponderance of evidence associates **evening chronotype** with elevated risk across multiple cancer types, though with meaningful heterogeneity:

- **Endometrial cancer:** Evening types had OR = 1.44 (95% CI: 1.09–1.91) vs. morning types in the California Teachers Study (n = 437 cases, 26,753 controls). A Spanish hospital-based case-control was inconsistent.
- **Breast cancer:** Evening chronotype associated with higher risk in multiple large cohort studies: California Teachers Study OR = 1.20 (95% CI: 1.06–1.35); prospective study HR = 1.19 (95% CI: 1.04–1.36); Alberta's Tomorrow Project HR = 1.49 (95% CI: 1.09–2.03) for breast specifically. One Nurses' Health Study II result was null for evening type per se (OR = 1.27 for "neither" type vs. definite morning).
- **Lung cancer:** UK Biobank (469,691 participants, 2177 cases) HR = 1.25 (95% CI: 1.07–1.46) for evening vs. morning; confirmed in a follow-up of 382,966 with 3644 cases (slight: HR = 1.17; definite evening: HR = 1.37).
- **Prostate cancer:** Finnish Twin Cohort (11,370 twins, 602 cases) HR = 1.3 (95% CI: 1.1–1.6) for "somewhat evening"; French case-control OR = 1.96 (95% CI: 1.04–3.70); but UK Biobank (213,999 individuals, 6,747 cases) reported no association.
- **Esophageal cancer:** UK Biobank (393,114 participants, 389 cases) HR = 2.79 (95% CI: 1.32–5.88) for evening chronotype (notable effect size, small case count).
- **Null / inconsistent results** for pancreatic cancer (UK Biobank, HR = 0.99) and ovarian cancer (population-based case-control, no consistent shift-work increase).

### Mendelian randomization evidence

Seven MR studies suggest a causal direction, providing stronger evidence than observational data alone:

- **Broad multi-cancer MR (UK Biobank, 50,252 total-cancer cases):** Definite morning chronotype protective for overall cancer (OR = 0.91, 95% CI: 0.85–0.97), lung (OR = 0.34, 95% CI: 0.26–0.44), breast (OR = 0.69, 95% CI: 0.59–0.80), ovarian (OR = 0.61, 95% CI: 0.39–0.97), endometrial (OR = 0.62, 95% CI: 0.43–0.91).
- **Breast cancer MR (133,384 cases, BCAC+DRIVE):** Genetically predicted morning chronotype OR = 0.89 (95% CI: 0.83–0.94).
- **Digestive tract cancer MR (UK Biobank + FinnGen):** Morning chronotype reduced risk of overall digestive (OR = 0.94), stomach (OR = 0.84), and colorectal cancer (OR = 0.92).
- **Prostate cancer MR (79,148 cases, PRACTICAL Consortium):** Genetically predicted morningness OR = 0.71 (95% CI: 0.54–0.94).
- **Lung cancer MR:** No significant association with chronotype per se; sleeplessness (not chronotype) was the relevant sleep trait.
- **Colon cancer MR:** Morning chronotype only marginally associated (OR = 1.004, 95% CI: 1.000–1.007; near null).

### Time-restricted eating and meal timing

Evidence for TRE per se in humans is sparse and indirect:

- **Prostate cancer:** Nighttime fasting >11 h associated with reduced risk (OR = 0.77, 95% CI: 0.54–1.07) in the MCC-Spain Study. Combining long nighttime fast with early breakfast further reduced risk (OR = 0.54, 95% CI: 0.27–1.04).
- **Breast cancer:** MCC-Spain Study found no association with nighttime fasting duration overall; in premenopausal women, each hour later breakfast was associated with an 18% increase in breast cancer risk (OR = 1.18, 95% CI: 1.01–1.40).
- **Late eating:** NutriNet-Santé cohort (41,389 day-working adults, 1732 incident cancers) found last eating episode after 9:30 PM associated with increased breast HR = 1.48 (95% CI: 1.02–2.17) and prostate HR = 2.20 (95% CI: 1.28–3.78) cancer risk. Nighttime fasting duration and total eating episodes were not independently associated.
- **Breakfast skipping** was associated with higher cancer mortality in a comprehensive literature review (referenced without primary data in this paper).

### Proposed mechanisms

Three mechanistic pathways are synthesized:

1. **Cell-cycle clock-gene regulation:** Circadian-controlled genes (WEE1, MYC, Cyclin D1/CDKN1A, p53/MDM2, GADD45a, VEGF, PDGFr) gate G1/S and G2/M transitions and DNA-damage checkpoints. Disruption uncouples these gates, leading to genomic instability and loss of apoptosis. TRE modulates mTOR and AMPK activity, promoting circadian autophagy, which suppresses tumour development in animal models.
2. **Metabolic/inflammatory pathways:** Evening eating or circadian misalignment exacerbates postprandial hyperglycaemia, hyperinsulinaemia, elevated IGF-1 availability, and chronic low-grade inflammation (IL-6, IL-1β, IL-17, TNF-α). Morning chronotype and early TRE improve insulin sensitivity, hepatic glycogen storage, and mitochondrial function. Clock genes (BMAL1, CRY1, CRY2) are upregulated by aligned feeding.
3. **Gut microbiota rhythmicity:** More than half of gut microbial composition fluctuates rhythmically with feeding-fasting cycles. Irregular meal timing disrupts microbiome oscillations (especially Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes diurnal phase), leading to gut barrier compromise, LPS translocation, and altered epigenetic regulation via SCFAs (butyrate) and methyl donors. *Alistipes putredinis* is more abundant in early chronotypes; *Lachnospira pectinoschiza* in late chronotypes. Family Bacteroidaceae, Parabacteroides, and Bacteroides are causally linked to morning chronotype in MR analyses. Whether these microbiome differences translate to cancer risk remains undemonstrated in humans.

## Limitations

1. **Narrative, not systematic:** No pre-specified search strategy, inclusion criteria, or risk-of-bias assessment. Evidence synthesis is selective.
2. **Residual confounding in observational studies:** Chronotype is correlated with shift work, sleep duration, diet quality, smoking, and socioeconomic status; adjustment varies across studies. The evening-chronotype cancer signal may partly reflect these covariates.
3. **Chronotype measurement heterogeneity:** Studies use single-item self-report (Horne-Östberg questionnaire items), Munich Chronotype Questionnaire, touchscreen self-report (UK Biobank), and sleep-midpoint proxies — not interchangeable.
4. **TRE human evidence is sparse and retrospective:** Only 4 studies in Table 3; most rely on retrospective dietary recall, none are RCTs for cancer incidence. The NutriNet-Santé "late eating" finding involves low incident cancer rates and borderline CIs.
5. **Mechanistic evidence is largely preclinical:** Cell-cycle, mTOR/AMPK, and gut microbiome mechanisms are drawn heavily from animal models. Direct causal evidence in humans linking TRE timing to circadian clock-gene expression in tumour tissue is absent.
6. **Cancer-type heterogeneity:** MR evidence is strongest for breast, lung, and endometrial cancers; colorectal, prostate, and pancreatic findings are mixed or null. The review does not stratify by molecular subtype (e.g., ER+ vs. ER- breast cancer).
7. **Sex and menopausal status underexplored:** The MCC-Spain meal-timing findings differ by menopausal status; most large cohort studies are sex-specific (female-focused). Interactions with hormonal state are not systematically addressed.
8. **Gut microbiome → cancer pathway is speculative:** The authors acknowledge that the microbiome-chronotype-cancer chain has not been demonstrated in humans; it is inferred from indirect evidence across separate literatures.
9. **No dose-response or threshold analysis** for TRE duration or meal timing.
10. **Esophageal cancer HR = 2.79** is based on only 389 cases; the confidence interval (1.32–5.88) is very wide and the finding requires replication.
