## Key Findings

### Molecular architecture

- The core TTFL involves CLOCK–BMAL1 (activators) and PER–CRY repressor complexes, with interlocking secondary loops via REV-ERBs/RORs controlling *BMAL1* transcription. A new component, RUVBL2 (a P-loop ATPase), has been identified as a core clock constituent coupling transcriptional and phosphorylation cycles; RUVBL2 variants produce arrhythmic, short-period and long-period phenotypes in mice.
- CRY1 and CRY2 have distinct functional properties: CRY1 binds the PAS domain core of CLOCK–BMAL1 with higher affinity than CRY2, and PER2 remodels CRY2 to increase its affinity — underscoring that the repressive complex is not stoichiometrically fixed.
- Single-cell SCN sequencing reveals spatiotemporal heterogeneity; PROK2–PROKR2 neuropeptide signalling maintains network rhythmicity; ZFHX3 is a genomic integrator coupling CLOCK function to neuropeptide coherence.
- Non-transcriptional redox rhythms (peroxiredoxin oxidation/reduction cycles, ~24 h) persist independently of the TTFL, representing intrinsic metabolic timing mechanisms that interact bidirectionally with the clock.
- The TTFL operates in a dynamic chromatin context: CLOCK–BMAL1 pioneers normally inaccessible E-box motifs from heterochromatin; circadian 3D genome topology (Hi-C/4C) shows rhythmic switching between active and inactive chromosomal compartments with oscillating promoter–enhancer contacts.

### Genomic scope and metabolic coupling

- Genome-wide mapping (mouse liver) shows ~1,444 loci co-occupied by BMAL1, CLOCK, PER1/2, CRY1/2, with hundreds to thousands of exclusive binding sites for CRY1, CRY2, PER2 enriched for nuclear receptor motifs. ~40% of protein-coding genes exhibit circadian transcription across 12 murine tissues; >80% of FDA-approved drug targets show circadian rhythmicity.
- A diurnal primate transcriptome (64 tissues, *Papio anubis*) found ~80% of protein-coding genes show daily rhythmic expression, with peak expression clustering around dawn and dusk.
- The clock tightly regulates the NAD+ salvage pathway (NAMPT), redox homeostasis (via SIRT3, PRX), hepatic gluconeogenesis (via CRY–GPCR–cAMP and REV-ERB–NCOR–HDAC3 axes), lipid metabolism (REV-ERBα–SREBP–*Insig2*), and gut-microbiome-dependent intestinal lipid absorption (via STAT3–NFIL3–HDAC3).
- Gut metabolite urolithin A bolsters clock amplitude through the Nrf2–SIRT1 axis; S-adenosylhomocysteine hydrolase (AHCY) rhythmically associates with CLOCK–BMAL1 to supply methyl-group donors for circadian transcriptional programmes.

### Genetic variation and disease risk

- Clock gene SNPs associate with a broad disease spectrum (Fig. 3): depression, obesity, breast/lung/liver/gastric/prostate/colorectal cancers, non-Hodgkin lymphoma, rheumatoid arthritis, Graves' disease, Alzheimer's disease.
- Key specific associations: BMAL1 rs7950226 + rs11022775 combined associate with type 2 diabetes (genotypic risk ratio 1.54, 95% CI: 1.22–1.97, P = 0.002); CRY1Δ11 (rs184039278) and PER2 Val205Met (rs76355956; OR 2.32, 95% CI: 1.18–4.53, P = 0.026) associate with delayed sleep phase disorder; PER1 rs3027178 confers reduced Alzheimer's disease risk (OR 0.803, 95% CI: 0.647–0.996, P = 0.046); PER2 rs2304672 links to morning preference and lower cognitive performance.
- Most reported links are correlative; directional effects are sometimes conflicting across studies.

### Circadian misalignment and chronic disease

- Shiftwork, social jet lag, and sleep deprivation compromise physical and mental health. Shift workers have elevated glucose, insulin, triacylglycerols, and inflammatory markers; weight gain vulnerability is higher in women, Asian workers, and individuals with obesity.
- Artificial light at night (>170,000 person meta-analysis) associates with increased breast cancer incidence (risk ratio 1.16, 95% CI: 1.04–1.28), especially in premenopausal, oestrogen receptor-positive women; thyroid cancer hazard ratio 1.81 (95% CI: 1.26–2.60, P_trend = 0.001) in women.
- Meal timing: late-night eating associates with higher breast cancer (OR 1.50, 95% CI: 1.06–2.12) and prostate cancer risk (HR 2.20, 95% CI: 1.28–3.78); earlier dinners and longer nightly fasting intervals reduce both risks. However, several recent meta-analyses report little/no significant association between night shiftwork and cancer specifically.
- Day-restricted feeding in mice drives diurnal mitochondrial proteome and lipidome rhythms in white adipose; adipocyte AMPKα2 is identified as a key regulator of circadian metabolic coordination between fat and muscle.

### Clock components in cancer

- Pan-cancer transcriptomics reveals widespread loss of coordinated core clock expression in tumours, correlating with poorer outcomes.
- TTFL components act as context-dependent tumour suppressors or oncogenic drivers: BMAL1 overexpression restrains tumour proliferation in colorectal and tongue cancers; *Bmal1* loss accelerates KRAS-driven lung adenocarcinoma and colorectal progression; CRY2-FBXL3-mediated MYC turnover suppresses lymphoma/colorectal/lung tumours.
- In haematological malignancies (AML, glioblastoma), CLOCK and BMAL1 sustain stemness and metabolic fitness and are required for leukaemia propagation in vivo.
- CLOCK–BMAL1 promotes homologous recombination and chemoresistance in adrenocortical carcinoma; CRY1 enhances HR-mediated survival in prostate cancer.
- Immune-clock interactions are elaborated in Box 3: morning dosing of immune checkpoint inhibitors improves survival; timed IFNα reduces toxicity; PDL1+ myeloid-derived suppressor cells oscillate daily, creating windows for more effective checkpoint blockade.

### Circadian-informed treatment strategies

- 'Drugging the clock': CK inhibitors (PF-670462, GO289), CRY stabilizers (KL001, SHP1705 — first to reach Phase I safety trials), REV-ERB agonists (SR9009, SR9011, SR12418, STL1267, SR29065), RORα/γ agonists and inverse agonists (SR1078, T1317, SR1001), natural ligands (nobiletin — metabolic, anti-tumour, Alzheimer's).
- 'Clocking the drug': Cancer chronotherapy exemplified by the EORTC 05011 trial confirming the 5-day chrono-IFL regimen — chronomodulated irinotecan (180 mg/m²), oxaliplatin (80 mg/m²), fluorouracil–leucovorin (2,800 and 1,200 mg/m²) — enhanced colorectal cancer survival while reducing toxicity vs. standard infusion.
- Anthracycline cardiotoxicity reduced by chronomodulated administration. Everolimus (mTOR inhibitor) pharmacokinetics vary by dosing time in mice, suggesting chronomodulated regimens could improve safety in transplant and cancer settings.
- Sex is flagged as an underincorporated biological variable: preclinical studies show males are nearly threefold more susceptible to everolimus-induced immunosuppression; clinical 5-FU/oxaliplatin data support sex-specific schedules to improve tolerability.

### Ageing and longevity (Box 2)

- Ageing perturbs the SCN (reduced rhythmic firing, dampened neuropeptide signalling, impaired photic entrainment), propagates to peripheral desynchrony, and results in a markedly remodelled rhythmic transcriptome (more genes lose rhythmicity with age).
- *Bmal1* loss causes premature ageing and muscle degeneration; restoring *Bmal1* in brain and skeletal muscle is required to prevent these defects. Time-restricted feeding can partially compensate for central clock loss.
- Circadian disruption in ageing brain promotes neurodegeneration: amyloid-β oligomers drive plaque deposition in non-human primates; in older adults, circadian disruption associates with elevated amyloid-β, tau burden, and reduced cognitive function.

## Limitations

- All genetic association findings reviewed are largely correlative; the review explicitly acknowledges that most links between circadian polymorphisms or gene-expression changes and disease phenotypes remain associative, not causal.
- The molecular clock architecture described is primarily from rodent and non-human primate studies; tissue-specific clock composition in humans remains poorly characterized.
- The TTFL framework is evolving: RUVBL2 was only recently identified and its integration with the canonical clock remains unclear; PER structural incorporation into BMAL1–CLOCK complexes is a recognized gap.
- Disease-specific models are underdeveloped; the review acknowledges the same clock component can be a tumour suppressor or oncogene depending on context — a challenge for precision-medicine translation.
- Sex is systematically underrepresented in both preclinical and clinical circadian studies; most foundational molecular work used male animals or mixed/unrecorded sex cohorts.
- The chemical toolbox for directly targeting CLOCK and BMAL1 activators (as opposed to CKs or nuclear hormone receptors) remains limited; most compounds act indirectly.
