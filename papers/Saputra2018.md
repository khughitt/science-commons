## Key Findings

- **Under constant dose, synergy wins.** Greater initial killing reduces sensitive-cell population size and delays opportunities for double resistance to arise.
- **Under constant efficacy, antagonism wins.** When initial sensitive-cell efficacy is held equal, antagonistic combinations give singly resistant cells less fitness reward, delaying double resistance and lethal relapse.
- **Pre-existing single resistance shortens time to double resistance.** The qualitative advantage of synergy or antagonism remains, but differences shrink as the starting resistant population grows.
- **Synergy can be a double-edged sword.** The same nonlinear interaction that makes two drugs powerful against fully sensitive cells creates a steep fitness gain when one drug is partially escaped.
- **The clinically relevant comparison depends on toxicity and dose constraints.** Real treatment sits between the idealized constant-dose and constant-efficacy extremes.

## Limitations

- The model is nonspatial and abstracts away microenvironment, immune effects, pharmacokinetics, and toxicity details.
- Resistance mechanisms are represented as discrete phenotypic classes with simple transition rules.
- Direct jumps from fully sensitive to double resistance are excluded by assumption.
- The authors do not identify specific drug pairs where the predicted antiresistance benefit is clinically established.
