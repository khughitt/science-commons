## Key Findings

**Epidemic calendar scope.** Seasonal cyclicity is documented for the vast majority of the 60+ diseases reviewed, spanning bacterial, viral, parasitic, and helminthic infections; both acute and chronic; both directly transmitted and vector-borne; and both neglected tropical diseases and high-income priority pathogens. Seasonality therefore cannot be viewed as a property of a small subset of "seasonal" diseases — it is effectively a universal feature.

**Four categories of seasonal drivers.** The paper organizes drivers into:
1. *Environmental factors* — abiotic conditions (temperature, humidity, precipitation, water salinity) that affect pathogen survival, host immune defense (e.g., temperature-dependent innate antiviral defense), vector and reservoir dynamics.
2. *Host behavior* — seasonal changes in human contact rates (e.g., school-term aggregation driving measles), mobility and migration, and risk behaviors; also seasonal behavior in nonhuman animal reservoirs.
3. *Host phenology* — endogenously driven annual cycles in host physiology, including seasonal immune restructuring, reproductive cycles, metabolic cycles of body condition, hibernation, and migration. Phenology operates on susceptibility, infectiousness, recovery rate, mortality, and symptom expression — seven distinct mechanisms of action relative to environmental or behavioral drivers.
4. *Exogenous biotic factors* — community-ecology effects: parasite–parasite interactions within hosts (co-infections) and ecological community composition of hosts, vectors, and reservoirs outside hosts.

**Disease-specific examples.** Representative pairings: influenza — winter seasonality driven by absolute humidity effects on transmission; measles — school-term aggregation plus multi-annual epidemic cycles from susceptible accumulation; polio — summer peak, driver(s) still unclear; malaria — vector seasonality plus geographic heterogeneity in seasonal strength; TB — ~24% more UK notifications in summer vs winter, correlated with sunshine/vitamin D; RSV — winter in temperate zones with less pronounced tropical seasonality; cholera — climate (warm months, flooding) and geographic gradient relative to equator; MERS-CoV — introductions seasonal due to camel calving season; dengue, Zika, yellow fever, West Nile — vector (Aedes/Culex) seasonality.

**Host phenology as underappreciated driver.** The review highlights that seasonal immune restructuring — an endogenously driven, phenologically programmed change in immune function across the year — has received less mechanistic attention than climate variables, yet may be particularly important for diseases with seasonal flare-ups/symptoms and remission/latency cycles (e.g., TB, yaws, herpes zoster). Seven mechanistic pathways are distinguished: susceptible recruitment (via reproduction), transmission rate, acquired immunity and recovery, waning immunity, natural mortality, symptomatology/pathology, and disease-induced mortality.

**Multi-annual epidemic cycles.** Seasonal transmission interacts with susceptible-pool dynamics to produce multi-annual epidemic intervals in some infections (pertussis, measles cycling every 2–4 years). This interaction means seasonal forcing is not sufficient alone to determine epidemic timing — demographic renewal of susceptibles is also required, and the interplay can produce resonance phenomena detectable in interannual variation.

**Causal identification challenge.** Because "everything is seasonal," spurious correlations between seasonal covariates and disease incidence are ubiquitous. The paper argues that long-term parallel data on both potential seasonal drivers and incidence, tested against mechanistic transmission models, is required to identify causal seasonal forcing. Interannual variation and anomalous epidemic years are identified as key evidence for distinguishing causal from coincidentally correlated drivers.

**Wildlife as a model system.** Wildlife populations represent an extreme of natural environmental exposure, offering a useful model for understanding phenological and ecological drivers with fewer behavioral confounds than human populations. Isolation in time (complete absence of transmission outside a discrete season, e.g., in some sexually transmitted parasites) is proposed as an especially tractable system for studying evolutionary consequences of seasonal forcing.

## Limitations

- Narrative synthesis across 60+ diseases means evidence quality is highly variable; many disease-specific seasonality claims rest on single studies with limited geographic scope (location noted in parentheses throughout Tables 1–4). For many infections the direction of causality (which driver is primary) remains unresolved.
- The framework is descriptive/taxonomic rather than quantitative; no meta-analysis of seasonal amplitude or phase consistency across studies.
- Human phenology is highlighted as understudied but not mechanistically elaborated — the paper notes that the study of human phenology is "relatively new" and defers mechanistic detail.
- Geographic heterogeneity in seasonal timing is acknowledged but not systematically modeled; most data originate from high-income country surveillance.
- The review predates COVID-19/SARS-CoV-2 seasonal dynamics, which have since added a major new data point on seasonality of respiratory viruses.
- Most evidence on seasonal mechanisms is correlational; the paper's own prescription (mechanistic transmission models confronted with long-term parallel data) has been applied to only a handful of pathogens.
