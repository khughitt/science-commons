## Key Findings

**Simulation benchmarks.**
- MO outperforms SCITE, SiFit, and SPhyR across nearly all scenarios in order accuracy and adjacent order accuracy. The advantage is most pronounced when the number of cells is large and when error rates are low-to-moderate.
- SiFit has consistently the worst order accuracy, largely because it classifies some mutations as errors rather than placing them on the phylogeny, reducing coverage of the inferred order.
- SCITE order accuracy degrades as mutation count increases and error rates are high.
- MO's credible set accuracy is higher than MAP accuracy when error rates are large or missing data is substantial, demonstrating the added value of the probabilistic uncertainty quantification.
- Under finite sites (scenarios 7–8, back-mutations allowed), MO retains higher accuracy than SiFit when back-mutation rates are low; at high back-mutation rates, neither method reliably identifies mutation locations — an acknowledged limitation of the infinite sites assumption underlying MO.
- Scalability (Scenario 9): MO continues to outperform competitors on 500–1,000 cell datasets; SCITE could not complete within a practical runtime on 10,000 sites datasets.

**Prostate cancer (P1 and P2).**
- TP53 mutation inferred to occur on the trunk with high posterior probability in P1, consistent with its role as a universal early driver; FOXP1 also trunk in P1; BRCA2 trunk in P2; ZFHX3 trunk in both.
- Mutations in prostate cancer–specific genes (higher frequency in prostate) tend to appear later (closer to tips) in both patients, while pan-cancer genes cluster at the trunk.
- Results agree with Su et al. 2018 and are consistent with cancer gene ordering reported in the broader literature.

**Metastatic colorectal cancer (CRC1 and CRC2).**
- CRC1: APC, KRAS, and TP53 inferred on the trunk with high posterior probability — concordant with Fearon–Vogelstein model and Powell et al. ordering of colorectal cancer initiation.
- CRC2: APC, NRAS, CDK4, and TP53 identified as shared trunk drivers; an independent lineage of primary diploid cells with parallel evolution (ALK, ATR, EPHB6, NR3C2, SPEN) identified.
- Metastasis-specific mutations (ZNF521, TRRAP, EYS in CRC1; FUS, HELZ, PRKCB in CRC2) placed on metastatic branches, recapitulating findings of Leung et al. 2017.
- One discrepancy with Leung et al.: MO places NR4A3 and FUS on a different metastatic lineage from TSHZ3 and PRKCB.

## Limitations

- **Infinite sites assumption by default.** The primary model assumes each mutation arises at most once on the phylogeny, precluding parallel or back-mutations. The finite sites sensitivity analysis shows degraded performance under high back-mutation rates, and the authors acknowledge this is a known weakness.
- **Requires a fixed input tree.** MO conditions on a pre-estimated tumor phylogeny; tree uncertainty is not propagated into mutation order uncertainty. When the estimated tree topology differs from the true tree (as in simulation scenarios with estimated vs. true trees), accuracy drops compared to the true-tree condition.
- **SCS scale still modest.** Empirical applications use 10–36 cells/mutations. Although Scenario 9 shows scalability to 1,000 cells, real clinical data at that scale with high error rates remain a challenge.
- **Independence assumption.** Mutations are assumed to evolve independently across sites. This is violated by epistatic interactions or by chromosomal events (CNVs, LOH) that co-occur on the same branch.
- **Binary/ternary only.** Copy-number variation and structural variants are not modeled; the method is restricted to point mutations (SNVs) encoded as binary or ternary states.
- **Single time point.** Like all SCS-based phylogenetic methods, MO analyzes a snapshot; longitudinal dynamics require additional assumptions or multi-timepoint data.
