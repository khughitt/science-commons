## One-Sentence Summary

Nam et al. build a signed disease-disease network from UK Biobank PheWAS SNP-effect directions, introduce a label-propagation comorbidity-scoring algorithm for signed graphs, and show that accounting for synergistic vs antagonistic SNP-effect alignment outperforms unsigned sharing-count networks in predicting EHR-validated comorbidity.

## Key Findings

1. **Signed DDN construction from PheWAS z-scores.** Using UK Biobank PheWAS summary statistics (PheCodes, N = 502,505), the authors represent each of 427 diseases as a vector of z-scores (beta/SE) over 39,382 genome-wide-significant SNPs (P < 1 × 10⁻⁴, after LD pruning to 588,711 independent SNPs). Pairwise disease similarity is computed as a signed cosine over shared-SNP subsets (Equation 1), yielding a signed disease-disease network (signed DDN) with edge weights in [−1, +1]. Positive weights = synergistic associations (shared SNPs have the same risk direction for both diseases); negative weights = antagonistic associations (shared SNPs have opposing directions).

2. **Network summary statistics.** The signed DDN contains 427 nodes and 9,223 edges: 6,209 synergistic (67%) and 3,014 antagonistic (33%), giving an overall network density of 10.14%. Within-category edges (same phenotype class, e.g. circulatory-to-circulatory) are predominantly synergistic, while cross-category edges account for most antagonistic relationships. Of 427 diseases, 364 feature both association types, 57 only synergistic, 3 only antagonistic, and 3 have no associations.

3. **Signed graph Laplacian SSL for comorbidity scoring.** Standard label-propagation algorithms assume unsigned graphs. The authors adapt the objective function (Equation 3–5) to a signed graph Laplacian that uses |w_ij| as the degree and sign(w_ij) as the direction, allowing comorbidity scores for unlabeled diseases to propagate positively from synergistically connected nodes and negatively from antagonistically connected nodes. The closed-form solution (Equation 5) produces a real-valued comorbidity score f per disease relative to an index disease of interest.

4. **Signed DDN outperforms unsigned DDN.** Comparing prediction performance across all 427 phenotypes (evaluated against EHR-derived phi-correlation and relative risk from UK Biobank hospitalization records), the signed DDN achieves a mean AUC of 0.601 vs 0.573 for the unsigned DDN, with the AUC distribution shifted higher. More strikingly, signed DDN yields 341/427 diseases with positive Spearman rank correlations (of which 259 are significant, ρ range 0.103–0.556, P < 0.05), compared to only 163 positively correlated and 53 significant in the unsigned DDN. Only 5 diseases favored the unsigned approach.

5. **Top-15 correlating diseases are circulatory-disease-dominated.** The 15 best-predicted diseases (Spearman ρ > 0.5, P < 0.05) include 11 circulatory-related conditions (e.g. congenital anomalies of great vessels, left/right bundle branch block, cardiac pacemaker, unstable angina, hypercholesterolemia, aortic valve disease, myocardial infarction, coronary atherosclerosis). For these, the signed DDN substantially outperforms the unsigned DDN; 4 of the 15 are non-significant in the unsigned DDN but highly significant under the signed approach.

6. **Coronary atherosclerosis case study: direct and inverse comorbidity.** Using coronary atherosclerosis (PheCode 411.4) as the index disease, the signed DDN ranks top-tier (Tier-1) comorbidities as myocardial infarction (score 1.000, EHR RR 13.05), other chronic ischemic heart disease (0.993), angina pectoris (0.929), and unstable angina (0.855). The algorithm correctly recovers the known HDL-C antagonism: macular degeneration appears in Tier-10 (inverse comorbidity), reflecting the opposing effects of HDL-C on CAD risk vs AMD risk. Among Tier-1 diseases, all are synergistically connected with coronary atherosclerosis and with each other.

7. **Comparison to LDSC-based genetic correlation DDN.** The authors build an alternative DDN using LDSC regression (genome-wide genetic correlations; 18,165 edges, density 19.97%). Despite being denser, the LDSC-DDN cannot assign comorbidity scores for 98/427 diseases (too underpowered for LDSC) and does not clearly outperform the signed DDN at comorbidity prediction. The signed DDN's use of only significantly associated SNPs (P < 1 × 10⁻⁴) enables robust disease interaction detection even for phenotypes with modest GWAS power.

## Limitations

- **Single-cohort (UK Biobank only).** UK Biobank participants are healthier-than-average volunteers; disease prevalence estimates and PheWAS summary statistics may not generalize to clinical populations. Sex-specific phenotypes were excluded; sex-stratified signed DDNs are flagged as future work.
- **P-value threshold is arbitrary.** The 1 × 10⁻⁴ significance cutoff for SNP inclusion is less stringent than genome-wide (5 × 10⁻⁸). The network structure changes with threshold, and the paper does not report sensitivity to threshold choice systematically.
- **No temporal or causal directionality.** Synergistic/antagonistic describes shared genetic-effect alignment, not which disease precedes or causes the other. The paper's "direct/inverse comorbidity" terminology can be confused with "one disease causes the other," but the method cannot resolve temporal or causal ordering.
- **Comorbidity score conflates genetic and epidemiological signal.** The signed cosine is sensitive to the number of shared SNPs (magnitude) as well as direction (sign). Diseases with very few shared SNPs may have high-magnitude scores driven by a single SNP, inflating apparent antagonism or synergism.
- **LDSC comparison is incomplete.** The comparison with LDSC-DDN is presented only briefly; 98 diseases are excluded from LDSC scoring and the relative performance is not systematically quantified across all 329 comparable diseases.
- **EHR validation is within UK Biobank.** The PheWAS summary statistics and EHR co-occurrence ground truth are both from UK Biobank, sharing the same participant population. Independence of training and validation data is partial, not complete.
- **PheCodes are not MeSH.** Direct integration with PubTator-derived MeSH disease similarity matrices requires a PheCode ↔ MeSH crosswalk, which is non-trivial and introduces mapping noise.
