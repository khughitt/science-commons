## Key Findings

- **Adaptive therapy relies on three core assumptions.** Resistance should be costly or competitively suppressible, resistant cells should be suppressible by sensitive cells, and therapy should preferentially reduce sensitive cells.
- **The best model is not always the most detailed model.** Patient data may not distinguish among mechanistic models; simple identifiable models can be more useful than complex underdetermined ones.
- **Spatial architecture and tumor ecology matter.** Competition depends on local arrangement, resources, tumor boundaries, and lesion structure, not just bulk sensitive/resistant fractions.
- **Phenotypic plasticity and environmentally mediated drug resistance can defeat simple AT logic.** Reversible, induced, or microenvironment-protected resistance weakens the assumption that sensitive competitors reliably suppress resistant cells.
- **Multi-drug adaptive therapy is combinatorially difficult.** Model-driven evolutionary cycling, double-bind, and extinction approaches may reduce this complexity, but trial evidence remains sparse.
- **Real-time patient prediction is feasible in some prostate settings but not yet general.** PSA-based adaptive prostate models achieved useful prediction in small studies, but biomarker quality and time resolution are load-bearing.
- **High tumor burden is a tradeoff, not a free benefit.** Competitive suppression may require maintaining burden near a density-dependent "sweet spot," but this risks symptoms, biomarker failure, further evolution, and new metastases.
- **Trial design must handle responder selection and control-arm comparability.** Nonrandomized adaptive trials can overstate benefit if responders are compared to broader controls.

## Limitations

- The paper is a review of open questions, not a primary test.
- The clinical evidence base is still concentrated in a few cancers, especially prostate cancer.
- Many recommendations require biomarkers and lesion-level measurements not available in routine oncology.
- It leaves unresolved how to choose model complexity under sparse data and how to standardize trial endpoints across cancer types.
