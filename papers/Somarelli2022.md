## Key Findings

### Core thesis

The paper diagnoses a "two truths" problem: remarkable mechanistic progress coexists with persistently poor outcomes for most patients with metastatic disease. The authors argue that this gap persists because discoveries are not embedded in an evolutionary framework and therefore cannot be reliably translated into durable interventions.

**Central proposal:** Treat the real-time evolutionary dynamics of cancer cell populations as a foundational hallmark — not an emergent property — and use evolutionary theory to organize research programs, diagnostics, and therapy design.

### The seven guiding questions (verbatim, compressed)

1. **Genetic diversity vs. full molecular diversification.** Can cancer evolution be understood through mutational signatures and copy-number alterations alone? When does cancer progress through successive microevolution vs. punctuated genomic macroevolution ("genome chaos")? Do analyses of genetic diversity need to incorporate both mutational- and plasticity-mediated mechanisms, as well as context-specific selection forces?

2. **Reductionist vs. systems-level integration.** How do we combine reductionist studies with systems-level approaches to identify novel prognostic, predictive, and therapeutic strategies? How important and actionable is tissue/TME characterization alongside genetic/epigenetic/transcriptomic changes?

3. **Multi-omic sufficiency.** Is a broad genomics perspective (genetics, epigenetics, transcriptomics, karyotype/aneuploidy) sufficient to understand cancer cell evolution? To what extent are mutations buffered or purged by the cell, tissue, or organismal physiology? What additional layers (proteomics, PTMs, metabolomics) are required?

4. **Cell-autonomous vs. tissue-level disease.** Is cancer primarily a disease of rogue, plastic, fast-evolving cells that acquire increasing evolvability and therapy resistance? Or is it also a tissue/organismal disease where tissue control of cancerous cells has failed? Can tissue control be restored? How do cancer–non-cancer cell interactions (immune, stromal) in the TME drive co-evolution, and how prevalent is reprogramming of distant organs?

5. **Metastasis biology and paired sampling.** What more is needed beyond genomics to understand metastatic niches, spread, and therapy resistance? Are there sufficient paired primary–metastasis sample studies? Can karyotype profiles predict macroevolution of cancer? How can circulating factors (cfDNA, EVs, CTCs) improve prediction and prevention of metastasis?

6. **Experimental models and in silico approaches.** How can current cancer models (organoids, etc.) be improved? How can in vitro insights translate more effectively to clinical oncology? What new experimental models are needed for evolutionary trajectories and cancer evolvability? To what extent can bioinformatics, mathematical, and in silico approaches replace or augment model systems?

7. **Therapy-driven evolution and adaptive strategies.** When do aggressive chemo-, targeted, or immunotherapies — when falling short of cure — lead to accelerating cancer evolution and therapy resistance? What strategies can slow cancer evolution? Are mono-therapies suboptimal or ill-advised as cancer extinction cures? Which evolutionary phases (punctuated macroevolution vs. stepwise Darwinian microevolution) should be targeted? How do we balance small increases in PFS against the risk of accelerating disease through stress-induced mutagenesis?

### Arguments the authors themselves advance (not merely pose)

- Evolvability is already an established hallmark of cancer (citing Nowell 1976 and Tlsty et al. 1989), but has been underappreciated in both research programs and clinical translation.
- Aggressive therapies short of cure actively accelerate clonal evolution, enhance evolvability, and promote metastasis (citing Woolston et al. 2021 on anti-EGFR resistance, and Ye et al. 2021 on therapy-induced genome chaos).
- Maximally tolerated dose monotherapies can inadvertently trigger and accelerate cancer progression and metastasis in certain patients — this is not merely theoretical.
- "Genome chaos" (Heng & Heng 2021) should be treated as a distinct macroevolutionary mode, not just a source of additional mutations, because it restructures the fitness landscape discontinuously.
- Tissue-level suppression of cancer is a real, restorable control mechanism, not merely a backdrop — restoring it could provide new prevention and therapeutic strategies.
- The field needs to move from studying cancer evolution as a separate niche discipline to integrating evolutionary logic into every aspect of cancer research and treatment.

## Limitations

- The paper is a short (4-page) perspectives piece: all seven questions are articulated at a high level of generality. No new data, methods, or quantitative arguments are provided.
- The question taxonomy is expert-consensus advocacy, not derived from a systematic review; selection of questions reflects the authorship group's research interests (genome chaos, ecology, therapy evolution, organoids).
- The paper does not operationalize how one would empirically answer any of the seven questions — no proposed study designs, datasets, or measurable outcomes are specified.
- "Evolvability as hallmark" is argued by assertion and by citation to the authors' own prior work (Somarelli et al. 2020 Evol Appl; ref 15); the case is not built from a new empirical base.
- The adaptive therapy vs. extinction cure trade-off is raised but not analyzed beyond naming it as an open question.
