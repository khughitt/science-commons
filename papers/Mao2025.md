## Key Findings

### Taxonomy of experimental methods

**Non-CRISPR (pre-2013 / classical):**
- Vital dyes — transient, non-heritable; useful only for early embryology.
- Retroviral gene transfer — long-term but random insertion site risk; used in hematopoietic stem cell work.
- Cre-LoxP recombination — heritable, cell-type-specific; requires transgenic engineering; no temporal resolution.
- GFP/fluorescent reporters — live-cell imaging; phototoxicity and detection constraints limit scalability.
- Naturally occurring somatic mutations (microsatellites, SNVs, chromatin) — retrospective, no experimental perturbation required; **applicable to human primary samples without transgenic requirement**; limited real-time dynamics.

**CRISPR-Cas9 based (post-2013):**
- Barcode-based (LINNAEUS, ScarTrace, CRISPR-UMI, scGESTALT, MARC1): introduce heritable synthetic scars accumulating over cell divisions; can be combined with scRNA-seq. LINNAEUS and scGESTALT demonstrated in zebrafish; MARC1 extended to human and mouse organs (lung, liver). Overlapping/saturated barcodes remain a noise source.
- Homing CRISPR (Homing CRISPR system, MARC1 mouse line): homing guide RNAs direct Cas9 to its own loci, generating diverse heritable barcodes in daughter cells; overcomes Cre-LoxP limitations in complex organs; demonstrated in mouse kidney and brain axis development.
- Epigenetic methods (EpiTrace, RETrace): dCas9 fused to epigenetic modifiers records lineage without altering DNA sequence; EpiTrace exploits clock-like chromatin accessibility loci; RETrace combines microsatellite lineage tracing with methylation profiling. Minimally invasive; **applicable to human samples without genetic engineering** (EpiTrace uses endogenous clock-like loci).
- SMALT: deaminase-based C→T mutation system; faster mutation rate than standard CRISPR; suited for complex multicellular tissue tracing.
- PRIME-Del / Bi-PE: prime editing variants providing higher precision and broader editing scope; expand combinatorial barcode space.

**Which systems work in human samples without transgenics:**
- Naturally occurring somatic mutations (SNVs, microsatellites): yes — retrospective only.
- Mitochondrial DNA mutations: yes — basis of mtscATAC-seq (Nuno2024 context); not catalogued separately here but implied under somatic mutation methods.
- EpiTrace: yes — operates on endogenous chromatin accessibility clock loci; applied to human samples in cited work [64].
- RETrace: yes — endogenous microsatellites + methylation; requires single-cell resolution but no transgenic manipulation.
- CRISPR-based systems (LINNAEUS, MARC1, scGESTALT, etc.): **require transgenic models or ex vivo engineering** (organoids, iPSCs); not applicable to primary human in vivo samples. Human organoid applications reported [36].

### Taxonomy of computational methods

**Non-velocity low-dimensional embedding (static snapshots):**
- Graph-based: Monocle (MST in PCA/reversed-graph space), Waterfall, TSCAN, Slingshot (stable multi-trajectory), PAGA (large datasets).
- Manifold learning: MARGARET (deep unsupervised), MIOFlow (optimal transport + manifold), DPT (diffusion maps), Wishbone (bifurcating, combines diffusion and KNN).
- Principal curves: Embeddr, SCORPIUS, ElPiGraph (elastic principal graphs for complex branching), scShaper (ensemble pseudotime).
- SNV/CNV-based tumor lineage tools: EXPANDS, MEDALT (single-copy number; predicts survival in TNBC), MEDICC2 (haplotype-specific CNVs; accommodates parallel evolution; validated in 2780 tumors), CONET (MCMC copy number event tree; excels at breakpoint detection).

**RNA velocity methods:**
- velocyto: spliced/unspliced ratio → future transcriptional state; widely used but constant-splicing-rate assumption limits applicability in heterogeneous tumor populations.
- scVelo: generalised dynamic model → more robust velocity in developmental and cancer contexts.
- dynverse: integrates RNA velocity with PCA/t-SNE/UMAP for trajectory visualization.
- CellRank: RNA velocity + trajectory inference; accounts for stochastic cell fate; pancreatic development application.
- PhyloVelo: integrates RNA velocity with experimental lineage information (CRISPR barcodes, lentiviral barcodes, immune repertoire) via monotonically expressed genes (MEGs); validated on 7 datasets; bridges the past–future gap that neither pure RNA velocity nor pure lineage barcoding alone addresses.

### Cancer applications highlighted

- CRISPR barcoding revealed genetic heterogeneity, metastatic routes, and drug resistance evolution [14–16, 136, 137].
- Lineage tracing + scRNA-seq identified subpopulations with distinct metastatic potential [13, 17].
- ClonMapper (CLL): combined clonal characterization and separation to profile thousands of clones; identified chemotherapy survival trajectories also seen in primary human cases.
- WILD-Seq: expressed barcodes + population bottlenecking in syngeneic mouse models; identifies clonal transcriptomics and differential clonal abundance before/after therapy; enables rational combination therapy design.
- EpiTrace: simultaneous lineage and epigenetic tracking; applied to hematopoiesis and tumor biology.
- SciClone / MEDICC2 / CONET: CNV-based tumor phylogenetics applicable to primary human samples.

### Open methodological challenges (Section 5)

1. **Accuracy of lineage reconstruction** — Overlapping/saturated barcodes and technical artifacts in CRISPR barcoding systems; noise in single-cell data produces ambiguous lineage assignments; achieving high-resolution trees remains difficult at scale.
2. **Temporal resolution** — Current experimental systems capture endpoint snapshots or require continuous labeling from a defined progenitor pool; RNA velocity assumes constant splicing rates (invalid in heterogeneous/rapidly evolving systems); no method reliably resolves the *timing* of state transitions with both single-cell resolution and temporal precision.
3. **Multi-omics integration** — Combining transcriptomic, epigenomic, and proteomic layers with lineage information requires specialized algorithms; data complexity and noise make validation difficult; most computational methods rely on assumptions that do not hold across cell types or experimental conditions.
4. **Scalability** — Tools offering single-cell resolution cannot track large populations over extended periods; tools suitable for whole-organ/organism scale sacrifice individual cell-level resolution; current tools struggle with data saturation or computational bottlenecks at whole-tissue scale.

### Future directions identified

- Integration of scRNA-seq with CRISPR barcoding (dual genetic + transcriptional capture per cell; [153, 154]).
- Multiplexed imaging and spatial transcriptomics to add spatial context to lineage maps [155, 156].
- Non-invasive/minimally perturbative labeling (synthetic biology / chemical biology approaches [157, 158]).
- Machine learning / deep learning for complex multi-omics lineage datasets [159–163].
- Standardized frameworks and data-sharing platforms for cross-lab validation.

## Limitations

- Review scope is broad (developmental biology, regenerative medicine, oncology) and does not focus specifically on cancer evolutionary dynamics; cancer-specific methods discussion is condensed into ~2 pages of a 14-page review.
- Does not discuss endogenous retrotransposon insertions as lineage markers (an increasingly used approach in human retrospective studies).
- mtDNA mutations as lineage markers (the basis of mtscATAC-seq, central to q044) are not treated as a distinct category — they fall implicitly under "naturally occurring somatic mutations" but are not named or reviewed separately.
- The review does not distinguish prospective from retrospective experimental systems explicitly, which is the critical axis for human applicability.
- RNA velocity limitations are acknowledged but the deeper criticism — that velocity assumes a common kinetic regime inappropriate for transcriptionally heterogeneous tumors — is stated but not developed.
- No quantitative benchmarking of methods against each other on cancer datasets; all comparisons are qualitative.
- Publication bias toward methods works and model-organism demonstrations; human primary tumor applications are examples, not systematic assessments.
