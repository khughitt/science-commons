## Key Findings

### Clinical phenotype is order-dependent in JAK2/TET2 MPNs
- JAK2-first patients presented in clinic more than a decade earlier than TET2-first patients (younger age at diagnosis).
- JAK2-first patients were more likely to present with polycythemia vera (PV) and showed relative expansion of megakaryocyte-erythroid progenitors (MEPs); TET2-first patients showed relative expansion of common myeloid progenitors (CMPs).
- JAK2-first patients had elevated thrombotic risk; TET2-first patients had lower thrombosis incidence.
- Thrombosis-free survival and disease subtype distributions differed significantly between the two subcohorts.

### Clonal architecture is order-dependent
- In TET2-first patients, the most evolved (double-mutant) clone was typically a minor subclone; single-mutant (TET2-only) HSCs dominated the HSPC compartment and were stable over months to years.
- In JAK2-first patients, the double-mutant clone was larger and more dominant in the HSPC compartment; JAK2-first clones showed greater homozygosity of JAK2 V617F.
- This runs counter to the expectation that the clone with the most mutations will be the most dominant.

### Same mutations, different biology at the single-cell level
- Single-cell assays of double-mutant HSCs (carrying both JAK2 and TET2 mutations) revealed distinct functional properties depending on which mutation came first.
- The number of progenitor cells generated by a double-mutant HSC was increased in JAK2-first patients and decreased in TET2-first patients — an intrinsic cellular difference despite identical genotype.
- Transcriptional profiling showed that JAK2 V617F gene expression consequences were altered by the presence of a prior TET2 mutation, consistent with an epigenetic reprogramming mechanism.

### Three proposed mechanisms for order effects
1. **Intrinsic epigenetic impact:** The first mutation alters chromatin accessibility at specific loci, preventing the second mutation from exerting its canonical transcriptional effects. In JAK2/TET2 patients, both TET2 and JAK2 have known roles in chromatin/histone modification, making epigenetic path-dependence mechanistically plausible.
2. **Altered target cell pool:** The first mutation creates a heterogeneous expansion of daughter cells in varied differentiation states; the second mutation then acts on this altered pool rather than on the original progenitor context. The effective "target cell" for the second hit differs between mutation orders.
3. **Extrinsic microenvironmental remodeling:** The first mutation generates a clonal population that secretes cytokines and creates cell–cell interactions that condition the microenvironment. When the double-mutant clone arises, it finds itself in a niche shaped by years of single-mutant cell expansion, which differs between mutation orders.

### Clinical implications flagged by the authors
- JAK2-first patients may be differentially responsive to JAK2 inhibitors relative to TET2-first patients (in vitro data cited; prospective trial needed).
- Mutation order should be incorporated into clinical trial design for MPN and potentially other hematological malignancies.
- Previous trials showing no significant therapeutic differences could be re-examined if mutation-order genomic data were available.

### Challenges for solid cancers
- Colony-forming assays that make order determination straightforward in MPNs are not available for solid tumors.
- By the time of diagnosis, solid tumor clonal architecture is typically dominated by a single subclone, making ancestral single-mutant clones difficult to identify.
- Multi-site sequencing and VAF-based inference can determine order in a subset of patients, but power limitations and LOH complicate interpretation.
- The authors note that DNMT3A mutations in MPNs appear to precede JAK2/MPL so consistently that the alternate order may be unobservable — possibly because one order drives clonal exhaustion before a tumor emerges.

## Limitations

- The primary empirical data come from a single study (Ortmann et al. 2015) in a single cancer type (MPNs). All mechanistic claims about solid cancers are extrapolative.
- Colony assay — the key method for unambiguous mutation order determination — is specific to hematopoietic cells; no analogous assay is available for epithelial or other solid cancers.
- The Ortmann 2015 cohort size is not stated explicitly in this review, but MPNs are relatively rare malignancies; cohort numbers are likely in the hundreds at most [UNVERIFIED: exact n for the JAK2-first vs. TET2-first subcohort comparison].
- The three proposed mechanisms (epigenetic, target-cell-pool, microenvironmental) are presented as a conceptual framework; direct experimental tests distinguishing them in human material are not provided.
- The paper cannot address whether mutation order effects persist through therapy or are erased by treatment-induced selection — a gap the authors acknowledge.
- Ascertainment bias is possible: if one mutation order causes clonal exhaustion before disease emergence, that order will be invisible in patient cohorts, creating the appearance of a universal ordering preference (as may be the case for DNMT3A preceding JAK2/MPL).
- Data entirely from hematological malignancies; generalizability to solid cancers is explicitly flagged as an open question requiring new experimental systems.
