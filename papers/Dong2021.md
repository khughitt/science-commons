## One-Sentence Summary

A systematic post-GWAS survey of 439 UK Biobank diseases finds 11,285 multimorbid disease pairs, nearly half of which share genetic components at the locus, network, or overall genetic architecture level, with intra-system pairs preferring shared loci while inter-system pairs preferring shared network-level pathways, and hub diseases mediating the majority of cross-system genetic multimorbidity modules.

## Key Findings

1. **Scale: 11,285 multimorbidities among 439 common diseases.** Using hospital inpatient records from 385,335 UK Biobank patients, a relative risk (RR > 1, Bonferroni-corrected P < 0.05) framework identifies the largest common-disease multimorbidity atlas to date. Diseases on average have 51 multimorbidity partners; prevalence positively predicts partner count (Pearson r = 0.69, P = 3.3e-64). The three most multimorbid diseases are hypertension (I10), hyperlipidemia (E78), and type 2 diabetes (E11).

2. **46% of multimorbidities are genetically interpretable.** Using matched GWAS summary statistics from the same UK Biobank subjects (GeneAtlas; 332 of 439 diseases), 46% of the 8,212 multimorbidities with genetic information available (3,766 pairs) share significant genetic components at one or more of five levels: SNPs (147 pairs), genes (1,463), PPIs (1,803), pathways (1,959), and/or genetic correlation (1,970 pairs). Multimorbidities are significantly more likely to share each type of genetic component than non-multimorbid disease pairs at all levels.

3. **Intra-system pairs preferentially share locus-level genetics; inter-system pairs share network-level genetics.** Within the 24 physiological categories, 9/24 intra-category combinations show significant shared SNP/gene components (P = 4.6e-4 for SNPs); 52/276 inter-category combinations share significant PPI/pathway components (P = 4.6e-17 and 8.3e-17). Genetic correlation rates do not differ between intra- and inter-category multimorbidities. This two-speed genetic architecture — pleiotropic loci for same-system disease pairs, converged biological networks for cross-system pairs — is a central organizing finding of the paper.

4. **HLA region dominates multimorbidity SNPs; noncoding RNA enrichment persists after HLA removal.** 73% of multimorbidity SNPs map to the HLA region (chr6:29,691,116-33,054,976). After removing HLA variants, multimorbidity SNPs remain overrepresented in noncoding RNA regions (P = 3.5e-47 and 1.2e-61) and carry higher CADD scores (P = 0.02 and 1.5e-15) than other disease SNPs and non-disease SNPs. The HLA-mediated signal is largest for autoimmune-related diseases (E03 hypothyroidism, J45 asthma, K90 intestinal malabsorption, E10 insulin-dependent diabetes, G35 multiple sclerosis).

5. **Multimorbidity genes converge on cell immunity, protein metabolism, and gene silencing.** Functional analysis of network-level gene overlaps reveals the top 10 PPIs shared by the most multimorbidities are enriched in GO terms for gene silencing and protein metabolism (localization, acetylation, ubiquitination, catabolism). The top 10 pathways shared by the largest numbers of multimorbidities are predominantly immune-related and account for 56% of pathway-interpretable multimorbidities — led by "cell adhesion molecules CAMs," "antigen processing and presentation," and "interferon gamma signaling." The top 10 SNPs/genes together interpret 23-34% of respective genetic-component multimorbidities.

6. **Multimorbidity genes are broadly expressed, not essential, and show higher pLI.** Only 17% of multimorbidity genes are essential (mouse lethal orthologs), similar to disease genes generally, consistent with Goh et al.'s Human Disease Network finding. However, multimorbidity genes show significantly higher loss-of-function intolerance (pLI; P = 0.01 vs. other disease genes, P = 3.6e-11 vs. non-disease genes) and are expressed in more tissues (P = 4.7e-4 and 8.1e-44), indicating functional pleiotropy and peripheral-interactome positioning important for normal physiology but not survival.

7. **Hub diseases and small-world multimorbidity modules.** Two multimorbidity networks are constructed: the LG network (locus-level shared SNPs/genes) and the NG network (network-level shared PPIs/pathways). Both follow power-law degree distributions and display small-world properties (LG: sigma = 1.15, average path length = 1.03; NG: similar). Louvain community detection identifies 8 LG modules (sizes 10-33 nodes) and 10 NG modules (sizes 6-49 nodes), each with featured physiological categories and local hub diseases. Seven and thirteen "universal hub diseases" (nodes connecting >25% of all nodes) are identified in the LG and NG networks respectively — including I10 (hypertension), which connects 80% of diseases in the NG network. Hub diseases mediate the majority of inter-category multimorbid links within modules.

8. **Drug-multimorbidity relationships suggest repurposing opportunities.** The shared genetic components of 8,458 drug-multimorbidity relationships are identified by linking drug targets to multimorbidity genes. Proof-of-concept cases: EDNRA (aspirin target) is shared by I20/I25 (angina/ischemic heart disease) — 65% of dual-diagnosed patients use aspirin. MAPT (Lansoprazole target, a proton-pump inhibitor) is shared by E66 (obesity) and J84 (interstitial pulmonary disease) — a potentially adverse relationship since lansoprazole has been reported to induce interstitial lung disease.

9. **Genetic correlation positively associates with RR and phenotype similarity.** Genetic correlation (rg) correlates with RR (r = 0.39, P = 1.9e-72) and with phenotype similarity (r = 0.22, P = 0.03), consistent with a genetic basis for many multimorbidity relationships. However, 24% of multimorbidities with significant genetic correlations (1,970 pairs) cannot be interpreted at locus or network level, pointing to unidentified genetic architecture (e.g., CNVs).

## Limitations

- **Sample size per disease pair is small relative to full EHR databases**: UKB has ~410,000 patients total; many individual disease pairs have very few co-diagnosed patients. This likely underestimates multimorbidity pairs for rarer diseases and is acknowledged by the authors.
- **GWAS coverage not complete**: Only 332/439 diseases have GeneAtlas GWAS summary statistics; the remaining 107 diseases are excluded from genetic analysis. Diseases without GWAS are likely rarer or less-studied, potentially biasing the genetic architecture findings toward well-studied diseases.
- **European ancestry only**: GeneAtlas and 1000G reference LD scores are European. LD structure differences across ancestries mean the genetic-component findings may not generalize. This matters for diseases with strong prevalence differences across ancestries.
- **Symmetric, undirected multimorbidity**: RR is symmetric; the paper cannot address whether disease A causes disease B or vice versa. This is a known blind spot (see Jensen et al.'s directed trajectory work) and is relevant to h02.
- **HLA dominance is a confound**: 73% of multimorbidity SNPs fall in the HLA region. While the authors show results hold after HLA removal, the HLA's extreme LD structure may still inflate apparent genetic overlap between autoimmune diseases. The locus-level findings for immune categories should be read with this in mind.
- **No time-window sensitivity reported as main result**: Disease co-occurrence is defined across the full follow-up period (25 years). The supplementary mentions time-window sensitivity (Table S13) but the main text does not discuss how results change at shorter windows. Directionality and temporality of disease onset are not captured.
- **CNVs and rare variants not included**: The paper explicitly notes that 24% of genetically-correlated multimorbidities cannot be interpreted at locus or network level and proposes CNVs as an unidentified component. Rare variant effects detectable only with larger samples are also missed.
- **Online database accessibility**: The multimorbidity database (https://multimorbidity.comp-sysbio.org) is listed as a resource but longevity of third-party academic databases is uncertain.
