## Key Findings

- **AT outperforms MTD most clearly in polymorphic tumors.** When sensitive and resistant populations coexist, drug holidays let sensitive cells rebound and suppress resistant cells.
- **Monomorphic quantitative resistance weakens AT's advantage.** If treatment-induced resistance evolves rapidly as a trait within one population, AT may provide little benefit.
- **Evolutionary speed has context-dependent effects.** Faster evolution shortens time to progression for both MTD and AT. In polymorphic tumors, the proportional advantage of AT can increase because MTD fails especially quickly.
- **Cost of resistance lengthens time to progression but is not strictly required.** Competition and population structure can make AT beneficial even without a strong resistance cost.
- **Switch thresholds matter.** Withholding therapy sooner can amplify AT benefit in the model, but clinical translation must balance tumor burden, symptoms, and metastasis risk.
- **Cancer therapy is analogous to resistance management in pests and pathogens.** The model is framed as a general problem of managing evolving populations under human control.

## Limitations

- The models are generic and not calibrated to a specific patient cohort.
- Tumor burden is the main observable; spatial lesions, immune response, toxicity, and multi-drug schedules are simplified.
- The monomorphic/polymorphic distinction is empirically difficult to infer from routine clinical data.
- The model explores predefined AT schedules rather than optimizing patient-specific schedules.
