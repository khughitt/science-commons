## Key Findings

1. **Directionality is pervasive and asymmetric.** For females, 7,135 (30.3%) of 23,562 directional pairs had increasing causal effects; 7,330 (31.1%) had decreasing effects; the remainder were non-significant. For males, 7,541 (29.6%) increasing, 7,941 (31.2%) decreasing. The high proportion of decreasing effects (one disease reducing the likelihood of another) is notable — it suggests protective cascades or negative multimorbidity paths alongside the expected pathological ones.

2. **Top influential diseases.** The top influential disease in females was hypertension (I10); in males, non-insulin dependent diabetes (E10), followed by hypertension (I10). Five diseases appear in the top-10 for both sexes: hypertension (I10), anaemias (D64), liver diseases (K76), peripheral vascular diseases (I73), and COPD (J44). Two sex-specific influential diseases in females were obstetric/childbirth conditions (O68, O70); for males, mental and behavioral disorders due to tobacco (F17), fluid/electrolyte and acid-base disorders (E87), hypertension (I95), and acute renal failure (N17) were additionally top-influential.

3. **Top influenced diseases.** Eight top-influenced diseases were common to both sexes: anxiety disorder (F41), disorders of fluid/electrolyte/acid-base (E87), functional intestinal disorders (K59), anaemias (D64), acute renal failure (N17), hypotension (I95), respiratory disorders (J98), and pneumonia (J18). Anaemias (D64) strongly increased many other diseases in both sexes; renal failure (N17) and hypotension (I95) each showed shared top-influential and top-influenced status in at least one sex.

4. **Quick vs. slow causal effects.** Top influential diseases (liver disease K76, chronic renal failure N18 in females; fluid/electrolyte disorders E87, acute renal failure N17, hypertension I95 in males) showed quick effects — >50% of subsequent disease occurrences within one year. Top influenced diseases showed longer time courses: >60% of disease occurrences between one and five years after the influential diagnosis.

5. **Bi-directional progress clusters significantly more than uni-directional.** Bi-directional one-step multimorbidity progress shows substantially greater clustering within and across ICD-10 chapters compared with uni-directional progress. Several chapter-pair combinations show statistically significant bi-directional cross-chapter co-occurrence (circulatory–respiratory, digestive–musculoskeletal, genital–skin chapters, among others; P_perm < 0.05). This implies that bi-directional disease pairs share underlying developmental mechanisms — neither disease is simply causing the other.

6. **Multimorbidity progress constellations.** In both sexes, the 10 (female) / 9 (male) identified constellations are biologically interpretable:
   - The **strong bi-directionally connected constellation** (constellation a, both sexes): a large cross-chapter cluster dominated by inflammatory-basis diseases (infections, anaemias, metabolic/electrolyte disorders, respiratory failure, renal failure, gastrointestinal diseases, liver disease). Both sexes share this hub constellation; the authors note inflammation as a likely conserved defense mechanism.
   - The **hypertension-asthma constellation**: includes hypertension (I10), asthma (J45), obesity (E66), lipoprotein metabolism disorders (E78), gastrooesophageal reflux, anxiety disorders (F41), and musculoskeletal/connective tissue diseases. Represents dysregulation of inflammatory response (not infection-caused).
   - The **COPD-circulatory constellation**: COPD (J44), bronchiectasis (J47), circulatory diseases, diabetes (E11), epilepsy (G40), cellulitis (L03), mental disorders due to alcohol (F10). In males only: mental disorders from tobacco (F17) and hemiplegia (G81).
   - The **neurological-central-nervous constellation** (females): migraine (G43), sleep disorders (G47). Males: neurological-peripheral-nervous constellation with nerve root and plexus compressions.
   - **Sex-specific constellations:** Females had two genital constellations — one dominated by childbirth-related conditions (O68, O70) and one by leiomyoma of the uterus (D25) and genitourinary diseases. Males had a diabetes constellation (E10, E14) instead.
   - The 10 **hub diseases** for female constellations: disorders of fluid/electrolyte/acid-base (E87), hypertension (I10), chronic ischaemic heart disease (I25), deforming dorsopathies (M43), gonarthrosis (M17), diverticular disease of intestine (K57), secondary malignant neoplasm of respiratory/digestive organs (C78), noninflammatory disorders of cervix uteri (N88), malignant neoplasm of skin (C44), senile cataract (H25). Hub diseases for males (9): acute renal failure (N17), chronic ischaemic heart disease (I25), intervertebral disk disorders (M51), diverticular disease of intestine (K57), secondary malignant neoplasm of lymph nodes (N40), hyperplasia of prostate (N40), malignant neoplasm of skin (C44), senile cataract (H25).

7. **Inflammation as a cross-cutting mechanism.** The most strongly connected constellations in both sexes involve diseases caused by inflammation from infection (anaemia as a defense mechanism against microbes, liver/renal/metabolic links). The hypertension-obesity-asthma constellation reflects inflammatory dysregulation. COPD-cardiovascular constellation reflects bacterial-induced inflammation, low-grade systemic inflammation in COPD/diabetes/cardiovascular disease. Mental health clusters can be attributed to pro-inflammatory cytokine theory (immune system subjugating the brain).

8. **Sex differences.** Patterns are broadly similar between sexes, but important differences exist. Sex-specific constellations include the female genital/childbirth cluster, and male tobacco-mental-COPD-diabetes linkages. Sleep disorders and hearing loss cluster within hypertension-obesity-asthma constellations in males, but musculoskeletal/connective tissue diseases cluster there in females, pointing to sex-differentiated mechanisms in Alzheimer's disease pathways (refs 51–54 in paper). Bladder vs. breast cancer and tobacco-related mental conditions are sex-specific constellation elements.

## Limitations

- **UK Biobank selection bias.** The cohort is UK-only and is known to under-represent socioeconomically disadvantaged groups (the "healthy volunteer" effect). Findings may not generalize to other countries or populations with different unmeasured confounders. Rare diseases were explicitly excluded.
- **Unmeasured confounding.** TMLE handles measured confounders comprehensively, but unobserved variables (early-childhood exposures, genetic predisposition not captured by ICD codes, treatment decisions, lifestyle changes after diagnosis) could bias effect estimates. The authors acknowledge this and frame findings as hypothesis-generating.
- **ICD-10 linearization artifacts.** ICD-10 assumes mutual exclusivity and exhaustive coverage, but many conditions span multiple chapters. Cross-chapter clustering tendencies could partly reflect miscoding rather than true biological cross-chapter relationships.
- **Incident vs. prevalent cases.** The study captures one-step multimorbidity (first time a disease is recorded after another). It does not address chronic disease severity trajectories, treatment response variation, or disease remission.
- **Disease prevalence threshold (>1%).** Rare diseases excluded; the resulting 190-disease universe heavily over-represents common chronic conditions. Rare genetic diseases — which often have the clearest mechanistic links and are most relevant to h01-molecular-truth-axis — are absent.
- **Causal interpretation requires caution.** TMLE estimates are observational despite the causal framing. Diseases associated through common unmeasured exposures (genetics, socioeconomic status) will produce spurious directed effects. The authors emphasize the findings are hypothesis-generating.
- **Sex binary only.** The analysis stratifies on a male/female binary; intersex and non-binary individuals are not addressed.
- **Code availability.** GitHub code exists but UK Biobank data requires application; replication is contingent on data access approval.
