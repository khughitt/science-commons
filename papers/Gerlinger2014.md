## Key Findings

### Branched vs. linear evolution
Conventional models assume successive clonal sweeps along a linear tree; multi-region sequencing across colon, breast, kidney, pancreas, prostate, brain, and other tumor types increasingly shows branched phylogenies with truncal (clonal) driver mutations and heterogeneous branch-specific alterations. Branched evolution is not universal — ER-positive breast cancers appear relatively homogeneous — but is widespread in solid tumors.

### Parallel evolution as evidence of constraint
Geographically separated subclones within the same tumor independently acquire mutations in the same gene or pathway (e.g., SWI/SNF complex members PBRM1 and ARID1A in ccRCC; Figure 1). This convergent evolution indicates that cancer evolutionary trajectories are constrained: not all paths are equally accessible, and certain functional targets are under persistent selection across spatial niches.

### Driver vs. passenger distinction is context-dependent
The fitness advantage of any mutation depends on the cellular microenvironment (gene/protein expression context, local ECM, hormones, exogenous therapies). A mutation that is neutral in one context may be advantageous in another. The driver/passenger boundary is therefore dynamic, not fixed at the time of acquisition.

### Expanding populations, drift, and "surfing"
Cancer cell populations are generally expanding rather than fixed in size. In expanding populations, genetic drift effects are magnified: deleterious mutants can "surf" the wave front, and clonal replacement need not occur even when advantageous mutations arise, favoring branching. This helps explain why ITH persists rather than being resolved by selective sweeps.

### Genomic instability: "just right" threshold
Elevated mutation rates provide more substrate for selection but very high rates reduce viability. A "just right" level of genomic instability is proposed to be selected for, providing sufficient variation without catastrophic mutational load. Genomic instability may be a late-onset phenotype in many sporadic cancers, contributing to progression rather than initiation.

### Macroevolutionary saltations: chromothripsis, chromoplexy, genome doubling
- **Chromothripsis**: catastrophic rearrangement of one or a few chromosomes in a single event; found in at least 2-3% of cancers and up to one-quarter of bone cancers. Can alter multiple cancer drivers simultaneously.
- **Chromoplexy**: chains of translocations across multiple chromosomes, inferred from prostate cancer genomics, arising from double-strand breaks.
- **Kataegis**: localized hypermutation at TpC dinucleotides, often co-occurring with chromothripsis; mediated by APOBEC cytidine deaminases.
- **Whole-genome doubling (WGD)**: tetraploidy acts as an intermediate stage toward aneuploidy; tolerates ongoing chromosomal instability; buffers deleterious TSG mutations (Figure 2); facilitates rapid genotypic diversification. WGD events are found in many cancer types that have returned to near-diploid state, suggesting cycles of polyploidization/depolyploidization. Parallels drawn to WGD events critical for eukaryotic evolution (Arabidopsis, yeast).

### Micro- vs. macroevolution spectrum
A pan-cancer analysis identified M-class tumors (point-mutation driven) and C-class tumors (copy-number driven), suggesting that some tumors are primarily understood through microevolutionary driver gene logic while others require the macroevolutionary lens.

### Cancer stem cells and evolution
Cancer stem cell (CSC) hierarchies introduce non-genetic (epigenetic) phenotypic ITH and constrain which cells are subject to Darwinian selection (only CSCs propagate heritably). Hierarchically organized tumors are predicted to be more heterogeneous due to reduced competition between subclones. CSC hierarchies and stochastic Darwinian models are not mutually exclusive.

### Therapy as evolutionary pressure
- Therapy induces a population bottleneck, reducing heterogeneity, but is typically followed by re-expansion with new subclone diversity (analogous to adaptive radiation after mass extinction).
- Pre-existing resistance subclones (e.g., EGFR T790M in NSCLC, BCR-ABL kinase domain mutations in CML, KRAS in anti-EGFR-treated colorectal cancer) can be present at low frequency before treatment and expand under selective pressure.
- Multiple parallel resistance mechanisms (e.g., up to four distinct activating KRAS mutations in a single patient) demonstrate polyclonal resistance arising from ITH.
- Treatment itself can be mutagenic: alkylating agents induce MSH6 mutations and hypermutation in gliomas at relapse, generating new mutational signatures distinct from the primary tumor.
- Myeloma case study: subclones that disappeared during treatment recurred after subsequent lines, showing that therapy shifts clonal dominance rather than eradicating subclones.
- BRAF inhibitor-resistant melanoma cells became dependent on the BRAF inhibitor, suggesting that resistance acquisition creates new therapeutic vulnerabilities.
- Adaptive therapy (preventing elimination of drug-sensitive clones) proposed as a strategy to delay resistant subclone dominance.

## Limitations

- Review-level synthesis: many empirical claims rest on a small number of case studies (especially the authors' own ccRCC cohorts). The prevalence of branched evolution, parallel evolution, and chromothripsis across cancer types is stated to be uncertain.
- The micro/macroevolution distinction, while conceptually useful, lacks a quantitative demarcation. The boundary between "large driver mutation" and "macroevolutionary saltation" is not formally defined.
- Cancer stem cell biology is treated briefly and the reconciliation with Darwinian models is largely deferred to cited theoretical work rather than resolved here.
- The adaptive therapy proposal is speculative at time of writing (2014); referenced model (Gatenby 2009) is computational.
- Mutational signature analysis was nascent (Alexandrov 2013 is among the newest citations); the review's treatment is necessarily provisional.
- No formal treatment of epigenetic inheritance or plasticity beyond CSC epimutations — phenotypic plasticity as a distinct evolutionary mechanism is not addressed.
- The "just right" genomic instability concept is supported by retrospective clinical data; causal direction is not established.
