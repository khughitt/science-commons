## Key Findings

### Virus seasonality patterns (Figure 2)

- **Winter viruses** (influenza, seasonal HCoV including OC43/HKU1/229E/NL63, RSV): peak November–March in Northern Hemisphere temperate regions.
- **All-year viruses** (adenovirus, HBoV): detected year-round.
- **Summer viruses** (non-rhinovirus enteroviruses): higher summer prevalence.
- **Type-specific patterns**: PIV3 peaks spring, PIV1 fall; hMPV spring; rhinovirus spring/fall.
- Viral interference between influenza A and rhinovirus creates non-overlapping peaks; proposed via IFN-mediated cross-protection and antibody-based mechanisms.

### Outdoor → indoor climate transmission (Section 2)

- Low outdoor AH in winter translates directly into low indoor relative humidity (RH, 10–40%) in centrally heated buildings at the 20–24°C thermal comfort zone.
- People spend approximately 90% of lifetime indoors; most transmission events occur indoors in enclosed spaces.
- Person-to-person contact rates on weekdays vs. weekends show that indoor crowding — not weather-driven outdoor avoidance — explains most of the seasonality signal in industrialised populations.

### Viral stability and transmission (Section 4, Table 1)

- Low RH (20–50%) maximises influenza virus stability; intermediate RH (40–60%) leads to inactivation; high RH (>60%) is optimal for stability in droplet nuclei but via different mechanisms.
- Guinea pig aerosol experiments: influenza transmission most efficient at low RH (~20–35%) at 20°C and improved at 5°C vs. 20°C.
- At intermediate RH (40–60%), ~77.5% of mice exposed to lethal aerosol survived, compared to mortality at 23% RH — consistent across multiple animal models.
- **Table 1 climate summary**: tropics (high AH, high indoor RH) → fomite/contact transmission predominates; temperate winter (low AH, low indoor RH) → aerosol transmission predominates.
- High ambient temperature (30°C) blocks aerosol but not contact transmission, explaining absence of aerosol influenza spread in tropical zones.

### Mucociliary clearance (MCC) impairment (Section 5.1.3)

- MCC requires proper hydration of double mucus layers (viscous and watery); dry air causes water loss from the watery periciliary layer, reduces its height, and immobilises cilia.
- Ciliary beat frequency declines as ambient temperature drops below 20°C; MCC undetectable at 5°C in ciliated cells from human nasal/tracheal tissue.
- Low RH (10% vs. 50%) decreases MCC and impairs viral clearance following influenza infection in mice (Kudo et al. 2019, ref. 88).
- Dry air simultaneously triggers TRPM8-dependent MUC5AC mucin hypersecretion (via cold receptor at <27°C) in human bronchial epithelial cells, resulting in paradoxical thickening that further impedes transport.

### Airway epithelial integrity (Section 5.1.2)

- Dry air causes immediate airway epithelial cilia loss, detachment, and tracheal inflammation in guinea pigs.
- Dry air exposure impairs epithelial cell repair in mouse lung after influenza infection.
- Disrupted epithelial integrity is proposed as a winter-specific vulnerability independent of mucus effects.

### Innate antiviral immunity (Section 5.2)

- **Temperature effect on IFN signalling:** Rhinovirus replicates preferentially at 33°C (nasal cavity temperature in cold air) vs. 37°C because innate IFN production is substantially lower at 33°C; MAVS knockout at 37°C rescues rhinovirus replication to 33°C levels, confirming IFN pathway dependence.
- Two IFN-independent antiviral mechanisms (apoptosis and RNase L) are also temperature-dependent and restrict rhinovirus at 37°C.
- **Humidity effect on ISG expression (key finding):** Mx1 mouse model at 10–20% RH shows impaired ISG expression throughout the lung — not just airway epithelium but all major lung cell types — following intranasal influenza infection. Single-cell RNA-seq of Mx1 lung tissue confirmed global ISG suppression. Three parallel mechanisms: (a) impaired MCC, (b) impaired tissue repair, (c) global ISG suppression. Mice at 10–20% RH succumb to influenza more rapidly than at 50% RH.

### Photoperiod, vitamin D, and innate immunity (Section 5.3)

- Short photoperiod (8 h light) reduces phagocytic activity and ROS production of granulocytes in Siberian hamsters compared to 16 h photoperiod; short photoperiod increases NK-cell cytotoxicity. This suggests divergent immunological effects of photoperiod on different innate effectors.
- Vitamin D biosynthesis depends on sunlight; winter deficiency is common at high latitudes.
- Vitamin D-deficient bone-marrow–derived macrophages show impaired maturation, reduced surface antigen production, and reduced lysosomal enzyme/H₂O₂ production in vitro.
- Short daylight is identified as an additional contributing factor to winter innate immune impairment, separate from humidity/temperature effects.

### Adaptive immunity (Section 5.4)

- High ambient temperature (36°C, mimicking heat-wave conditions) impairs virus-specific CD8+ T-cell responses and antibody production in mice after intranasal influenza infection.
- Impaired adaptive immunity at high temperature is partially rescued by glucose or short-chain fatty acid supplementation, implicating diet and microbiome as modulators.
- All six recent influenza pandemics in the Northern Hemisphere (1957–2009) emerged in spring-to-summer (CDC surveillance), consistent with reduced herd immunity to novel strains when adaptive responses are highest.
- Housing temperature modulates anti-tumour and graft-versus-host immunity in mice, indicating systemic effects of ambient temperature on adaptive immune responses beyond the respiratory context.

### Disease tolerance (Section 6)

- Low humidity exposure in Mx1 mice increases mortality, weight loss, and pulmonary viral burden, and caspase-1/11 deficiency rescues disease in dry-air–exposed infected mice.
- Mechanism: dry air impairs innate resistance but allows tissue damage to be mediated by inflammasome-driven neutrophil activation; caspase inhibition restores disease tolerance independently of viral clearance.
- Potential therapeutic window: interfering with inflammasome caspases may counter deleterious consequences of influenza in settings with impaired innate immunity (elderly, dry environments).

### SARS-CoV-2 (Section 7)

- SARS-CoV-2 receptor ACE2 is concentrated in type II alveolar cells — not accessible to droplets >5 µm; severe COVID-19 pneumonia cases likely involve airborne (aerosol) transmission events.
- Winter conditions (low AH, low temperature) are predicted to enhance SARS-CoV-2 droplet stability, impair ciliary clearance, and suppress innate immune defences, consistent with early emergence in winter Wuhan.

### Nonpharmaceutical interventions (Table 2)

Humidification of indoor air to 40–60% RH; ventilation; face masks; vitamin D supplementation; sleep >7 h/day; hand washing.

## Limitations

- All mechanistic findings on humidity, temperature, and immunity derive from animal models (primarily mice, guinea pigs, hamsters); direct human experimental data on MCC impairment and ISG suppression by humidity are limited to in vitro cell culture or indirect evidence.
- Most ISG/MCC data used Mx1 transgenic mice carrying a human-like antiviral ISG; generalisation to standard immunocompetent mouse strains and to humans requires caution.
- The paper was published March 2020 and thus treats SARS-CoV-2 as emerging/preliminary — all COVID-19 claims are early-pandemic projections, not confirmed findings.
- Photoperiod/melatonin and vitamin D effects on innate immunity are described separately without integration into a unified seasonal immunity model; it is unclear whether they are additive, synergistic, or redundant.
- The paper focuses almost entirely on temperate-region Northern Hemisphere winters; tropical, Southern Hemisphere, and polar seasonality are addressed only briefly.
- Human intervention data for humidification come from older studies (1960s–1980s) with limited controls; one modern RCT (Reiman et al. 2018) supports humidification in preschool classrooms but evidence for adults in standard settings remains thin.
- No data on sex differences in seasonal immune responses or seasonal virus susceptibility are discussed; menstrual-cycle or reproductive-stage interactions with seasonal immunity are not considered.
