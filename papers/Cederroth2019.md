## Key Findings

**Circadian biology underpins most physiology.** Roughly half of all human protein-coding genes are clock-regulated. Clock genes are mutated in multiple diseases (Alzheimer's, schizophrenia, type 2 diabetes, cancer). The TTFL mechanism is evolutionarily conserved.

**Translational gap: nocturnal rodents tested at the wrong time.** ~95% of US laboratory animals are mice or rats, which are nocturnal. Researchers conducting experiments during daytime human working hours are testing at the rodents' nighttime — the phase when they are least active and most physiologically different from awake, active humans. The authors identify three specific amplifying differences:
1. Rodents are polyphasic sleepers with short sleep bouts throughout day and night; no direct human equivalent.
2. Rodents fast poorly — a single day of fasting equals ~20% body weight loss.
3. Temperature sensitivity is extreme — 1°C ambient change produces large shifts in food intake.

**Peripheral clocks are feeding-entrained, not only SCN-driven.** Insulin/IGF-1 signalling drives PER synthesis and can entrain peripheral circadian rhythms independent of photic input. The SCN-to-periphery relationship in nocturnal vs. diurnal species shows SCN clock phases are comparable, but peripheral oscillators are phase-opposed — so drug effects that depend on peripheral-tissue clock state (hepatic metabolism, kidney excretion, cardiovascular targets) are systematically misread when nocturnal rodent results are extrapolated to humans without phase correction.

**Circadian pharmacokinetics (chronoPK).** ADME properties of drugs are clock-regulated: gastric emptying, blood flow, xenobiotic efflux by blood-brain barrier, phase I/II detoxification via CYP450/SULT/UGT enzymes (controlled by CLOCK/BMAL1), and renal excretion. These vary substantially across the 24 h cycle. For drugs with narrow therapeutic windows or strong PK/pharmacodynamic (PD) correlation with a circadian target, timing of administration can profoundly shift the therapeutic-to-toxic ratio.

**Drug efficacy depends on time of administration.** Table 1 compiles 22 drugs studied in n > 100 clinical trials. Majority of antihypertensives (ACE inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, calcium channel blockers, diuretics) show best efficacy with evening administration. Corticosteroids (prednisone for rheumatoid arthritis) are more effective in the morning. Some show no difference (aspirin in one trial, statins in one trial — but a different statin study favours evening). The oncology example: chronomodulated infusion of oxaliplatin/5-FU/leucovorin achieved objective response in 51% vs. 29% for constant-rate infusion, and reduced severe mucosal toxicity (14% vs. 76%) in a Phase III RCT of 842 patients with metastatic colorectal cancer.

**Circadian disruption as a disease mechanism (not only confound).** Shift work correlates with metabolic, cardiovascular, inflammatory, and neoplastic disease risk. Reduced rhythm coherence may be a hallmark of neurodegeneration. In cancer patients (pooled analysis, 1,077 patients), circadian disruption was associated with significantly worse survival and quality of life.

**Targeting the clock system pharmacologically.** Most top-selling drugs have circadian targets with expression half-lives < 6 h, making timing critical for short-acting drugs. Small-molecule clock-modifying compounds (cryptochrome activators/stabilisers, REV-ERB agonists, CK1 inhibitors) are under investigation. The paper identifies three distinct intervention modes: (1) time the drug to match rhythmic target availability; (2) suppress or dampen clock output in the target tissue; (3) enhance or stabilise clock function as a "do no harm" mechanism (relevant for cancer, neurodegeneration, wound healing).

**Circadian alignment as a research and clinical goal.** The authors advocate that circadian factors should be recorded and controlled in clinical trial design — including chronotype, time of collection, sleep, and light exposure — and used for patient stratification. They call for circadian-omics-based phase translation maps, circadian biomarker monitoring in clinical trials, and chronopharmacology education in medical schools.

## Limitations

- As a perspective article, the paper argues for a field direction; the evidence cited is selectively marshalled. Not a systematic review.
- Table 1 drug studies are heterogeneous in design and patient populations; the authors note this explicitly, citing De Georgi et al. (2013) as the source and acknowledging the variability.
- The claim that oxaliplatin is "the only well-documented example" of a misleading clinical trial linked to circadian factors is a strong assertion that may reflect publication or ascertainment bias rather than true rarity.
- The meta-analysis showing improved survival with oxaliplatin chronomodulation (Giacchetti et al. 2012) was in men only — the sex-specific effect is noted briefly but not explained; this is a significant limitation for generalising the finding.
- Circadian biomarker monitoring ("chronotype" of a patient) is advocated as necessary but no validated clinical method is specified; this remains an open measurement problem.
- Most mechanistic evidence is from nocturnal rodents — the paper acknowledges this is the core problem it is trying to solve, creating some circularity in the evidence base.
