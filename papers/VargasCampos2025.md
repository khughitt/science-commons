## Key Findings

1. **Seasonality is broadly present across mycobacterioses.** Mean epidemic curve score across all 122 studies was 2.0 (± 0.9); TB studies mean 2.1 (± 0.6); MU/BU studies mean 2.6 (± 0.4), indicating moderate-to-strong seasonal patterns across major disease categories.

2. **TB seasonality: spring peak, latitude-dependent.** TB seasonal trends were most pronounced between 20° and 30° North latitude. Peak incidence fell predominantly in April/May (median peak May, IQR 2.5 months). TB accounted for 77.9% (n = 95) of included studies; China produced 40.2% (n = 39) of TB-seasonality publications, largely due to its national TBIMS surveillance system.

3. **Buruli ulcer/MU: July–October peak in humans, ecosystem dynamics.** Human BU cases peaked between July and October (median August, IQR 1.75 months); the environmental *M. ulcerans* load peaks 2–3 months before human cases — a transmission lag consistent with environmental exposure pathways. MU/BU studies adopted an ecosystem-based approach integrating environmental pathogen dynamics with case data.

4. **Bovine tuberculosis (bTB): September peak, wildlife/agricultural drivers.** bTB showed a seasonal peak primarily in September, coinciding with agricultural and wildlife management practices. Badger interactions, reproductive/breeding cycles of wildlife reservoir hosts, and indoor crowding in winter all modulate bTB dynamics. Advanced time-series methods were underutilized in bTB research (only n = 2 of 6 studies used them).

5. **Method sophistication directly predicts detection of seasonality.** Linear regression of seasonal curve shape against methodological score was highly significant for TB studies (r² = 0.3, F = 16.9, P < 0.0001); the trend was positive but weaker for other mycobacteria studies (F = 3.5, P = 0.07). Average method score across all studies was 1.86 (± 0.7). The most common advanced methods: ARIMA/SARIMA (n = 21), GLMs (n = 14), ACF/PACF (n = 19), mathematical modelling (n = 16), LOWESS (n = 9).

6. **Geographic bias: northern hemisphere dominance, tropical underrepresentation.** Research was heavily concentrated in temperate northern-hemisphere regions, especially China (TB) and specific tropical zones for MU/BU (Cameroon, Benin, Ghana, Australia). Regions above 40° North and the southern hemisphere were substantially underrepresented despite carrying high TB and BU burden.

7. **TB-specific confounds — latency and surveillance delay.** TB's long and variable latency period means the seasonal signal in reported cases may be displaced by months or years from actual transmission events. Surveillance systems capture a mix of recently transmitted and reactivated latent infections, both of which deviate from simple seasonal transmission curves. Reporting delays further blur apparent seasonal peaks.

8. **Climate, vitamin D, and host immunity as candidate drivers.** Discussion highlights three mechanistic pathways: (a) bTB intensifies in cold seasons due to indoor crowding and reduced ventilation; (b) winter vitamin D deficiency impairs immune responses to TB; (c) a speculative mechanism posits that elevated CD4 T-cell levels during winter delay TB symptom onset until spring, producing spring/summer incidence peaks. [SPECULATION: these mechanistic attributions are from the paper's Discussion and are not directly tested in the bibliometric review itself.]

9. **Two general conclusions for future research.** First, advanced statistical/mathematical tools from fields outside traditional medicine and microbiology (disease ecology, population dynamics, physics) are required to reveal seasonal dynamics across mycobacterioses. Second, MU/BU research demonstrates the value of an ecosystem-based framework integrating environmental pathogen load with human case incidence — a model proposed as applicable to other mycobacterial diseases.

## Limitations

- **Bibliometric review design: correlation between method quality and seasonal detection is circular.** Studies using advanced methods are more likely to find seasonality; it is impossible to know from this review alone whether seasonality is truly absent where only descriptive methods were used, or whether it exists and was missed. The review acknowledges this but cannot resolve it.
- **Heterogeneous outcome definitions:** Included studies vary in how "seasonality" is operationalized — incidence, prevalence, environmental load, diagnostic positivity, notification rates — making cross-disease and cross-region comparisons difficult.
- **Language restriction:** Exclusion of non-English/French/Spanish publications likely underrepresents South America, sub-Saharan Africa, Central Asia, and China's non-Mandarin-accessible literature.
- **No control disease included.** The authors acknowledge the absence of a low-seasonality comparator disease, which limits attribution of observed patterns to mycobacteria-specific biology versus generic surveillance artefact.
- **Mechanistic drivers not empirically tested.** The candidate mechanisms (vitamin D, indoor crowding, CD4 T-cell seasonality, environmental MU dynamics) are synthesized from the literature but are not directly evaluated within the review.
- **bTB and NTM underrepresented methodologically.** bTB and other NTM studies used predominantly descriptive statistics; the review's conclusion that advanced methods reveal seasonality rests largely on TB evidence.
- **Latency confound in TB signals.** The long incubation and reactivation mixture means that observed seasonal peaks may reflect reporting dynamics, health-care seeking, or latent-infection reactivation rather than primary transmission seasonality — a major unresolved issue for inferring true biological seasonality.
