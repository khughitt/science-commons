## Key Findings

- An optimal second-strike lag exists: extinction probability is low when the second strike is immediate, rises to a peak, and then falls as the population rebounds.
- In the baseline simulations, the optimal lag is relatively insensitive to mutation rate, but higher mutation rates reduce extinction probabilities and narrow the acceptable timing window.
- Using the more effective drug first improves extinction probability more than saving the stronger drug for the second strike.
- Combination therapy outperforms optimally timed sequential therapy when cross-resistance is absent, even when the individual drugs in the sequential regimen are stronger.
- When cross-resistance can arise, optimally timed sequential therapy can outperform combination therapy at tested low combination doses because first-strike selection can deplete variants resistant to the second strike.
- Equal drug doses in combination therapy maximize extinction probability under a fixed combined kill effect, even when the mutation rates to resistance differ between drugs.
- Qualitative two-strike dynamics persist when resistance is modeled as a quantitative multilocus trait, though the timing peak is broader than in the binary-resistance model.

## Limitations

The paper is a preprint and the model is intentionally simplified.
The simulated starting population is much smaller than a clinically detectable tumor, spatial heterogeneity is absent, and the baseline model omits Allee effects, pharmacokinetics, toxicity, immune effects, and non-genetic refugia.
The conclusion that the stronger drug should be first contrasts with Patil2025's more nuanced result for wider timing windows, so this should be treated as model-contingent rather than universal.
