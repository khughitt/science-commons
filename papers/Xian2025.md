## One-Sentence Summary

An unsupervised three-step transformer pipeline (vocabulary autoencoder → transformer patient sequences → sentence-BERT aggregation) trained on 102,740 eMERGE patients produces patient embeddings that simultaneously support disease onset prediction (median AUROC = 0.87), bulk phenotyping (median AUROC = 0.84), and comorbidity-subtype clustering with distinct progression trajectories and survival differences — validated externally on 840,000 UW patients.

## Key Findings

1. **Three-step embedding architecture achieves strong predictive performance without fine-tuning.** Vocabularies (ICD-9, ICD-10, CPT codes) are first encoded by an autoencoder compressing 4,320 onset-frequency features to 50 dimensions. Codes are then fed as sentence tokens into a transformer model (annual visits as "sentences," codes as "words"), producing longitudinal patient vectors. Finally a sentence-BERT layer creates a fixed-length patient embedding. No disease labels or domain expertise are required during training.

2. **Disease onset prediction: median AUROC = 0.87 across 1,855 phenotypes.** Using patient vectors one year before diagnosis as input to simple logistic regression, the model achieves median AUROC = 0.87 for future disease prediction. The best-performing pheCode system is pregnancy complications (AUROC = 0.93); the weakest is congenital anomalies (AUROC = 0.82). Outperforms or matches the specialist models Deep Patient and BEHRT on the same task (BEHRT: 0.022 lower median AUROC, 0.012 lower mean AUROC).

3. **Bulk phenotyping of 1,855 phecode classes: median AUROC = 0.84.** Logistic regression on summed (time-averaged) embeddings distinguishes disease state from non-disease state without any label-based fine-tuning. The circulatory system class achieves the highest AUROC; pregnancy complications the best overall. AUPRC strongly correlates with positive-to-negative ratio (Spearman r = 0.87, p < 1e−200), confirming performance scales gracefully with class prevalence.

4. **Sentence-embedding model validates patient identity and temporal continuity.** For the identity task (`is_same_patient`), accuracy is 0.797 ± 0.0016; for the event-transition task (`is_next_event`), accuracy is 0.769 ± 0.011. External UW validation yields 0.796 ± 0.0023 and 0.742 ± 0.0042, respectively — only minor degradation, confirming generalizability of the representation.

5. **Comorbidity sub-phenotyping within CRC (n = 2,837 eMERGE) identifies 4 clusters with distinct onset ages and survival.** Gaussian mixture model (GMM) on BIC-optimal k = 4 separates: Cluster 2 (youngest onset, median age ≈ 51, HIV/STI-enriched; significantly lower 10-year survival, Kaplan-Meier p ≈ 4.72e−93) from Clusters 0/1/3 (older onset, secondary malignancy burden, cardiovascular comorbidities). Cluster 2 reproduced as the "early-onset infectious-disease-enriched" subgroup in UW validation (n = 3,312). Cluster 1 in eMERGE represents cancer pleiotropy with late-stage metastatic features.

6. **Comorbidity sub-phenotyping within SLE (n = 1,806 eMERGE) identifies 4 clinically distinct clusters.** Cluster 0 (youngest onset, median age = 37, epilepsy-associated); Cluster 1 (oldest onset, median age = 57, dermatologic and eye disease — cataracts, glaucoma); Cluster 2 (fertility/menstrual/pregnancy-complication enriched, median age = 40); Cluster 3 (renal axis — nephropathy, dialysis, end-stage renal disease, median age = 44). SLE clusters reproduced in UW (n = 2,546), with survival differences (cluster 1 lowest 10-year survival, FDR = 8.01e−4). Male-enriched cluster 1 is consistent with prior reports of lower life expectancy in male SLE patients.

7. **Longitudinal progression trajectories diverge across CRC sub-clusters before diagnosis.** PCA on longitudinal CRC patient vectors (n = 110, 10-year follow-up) shows PC1/PC2 are explained by cluster identity and age/sex/sites; PC3 by sites alone. The initial pre-diagnosis frequency of shared comorbidities across all four clusters is only 21%, rising to 55% after onset — indicating divergence begins before clinical diagnosis and progression is highly heterogeneous.

8. **External validation on UW (n = 840,000) reproduces key metrics.** Sequence-recovery precision = 0.862 ± 0.129 / recall = 0.852 ± 0.128 (vs eMERGE precision = 0.917, recall = 0.895). Bulk phenotyping AUROC = 0.83, disease onset AUROC = 0.84. CRC and SLE sub-clusters reproduced with similar composition and comorbidity enrichment patterns; survival differences confirmed.

## Limitations

- **EHR codes only — no genotype, no molecular data.** Comorbidity clusters reflect shared clinical coding patterns. An HIV-enriched CRC cluster is a framing/practice artifact as much as a biological subtype. The method cannot distinguish shared biology from shared clinical context.
- **12-site eMERGE training may carry site-specific coding practice.** PC3 of the longitudinal CRC vectors is explained primarily by sites, not biology — the authors acknowledge this explicitly.
- **Phecode mapping granularity.** Phecode binning of ICD codes introduces its own taxonomy choices; some biologically distinct ICD entities are merged. Mapping to MeSH (for comparison with PubTator axes) would require an additional crosswalk and is not provided.
- **No molecular ground truth.** Unlike h01's approach (which tries to anchor clusters to DEGs or curated gene panels), Xian2025 has no way to distinguish a clustering that reflects shared biology from one that reflects shared socioeconomic factors, care-access patterns, or geographic coding variation.
- **Short sequence problem.** The transformer performs worse for patients with sparse visit histories — the attention mechanism requires co-occurrence patterns within a year, so rare-encounter patients are poorly represented.
- **Supervised comparisons (BEHRT, Deep Patient) may not be fair.** BEHRT is fine-tuned for disease prediction; Xian2025's embedding is general-purpose; that the general model nearly matches the specialist is the paper's point, but direct AUROC comparisons are confounded by training objective differences.
