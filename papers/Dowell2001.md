## Key Findings

### Epidemiological anomalies that motivate the hypothesis

- Different pathogens peak reliably in each of the four seasons, with annual timing varying by only 5–10 weeks for influenza in the US.
- Epidemics in geographically dispersed areas (different weather, populations) begin simultaneously — documented for influenza (North America + 16 European countries + 6 Chinese provinces), pneumococcus (7 US surveillance sites), and in historical accounts as far back as 1826.
- Pathogens persist in the off-season without epidemic spread: meningococcus carriage does not decrease in summer; RSV and influenza show sporadic summer isolations.
- Latitude strongly modulates peak timing and magnitude for polio, rotavirus, and influenza, with more extreme seasonal peaks farther from the equator.
- Weather correlations are inconsistent across sites (e.g. RSV peaks in cold months in the US but hot months in Singapore/Hong Kong; meningococcus peaks during dry season in sub-Saharan Africa but rainy season in Oregon).

### The host-susceptibility hypothesis

- Annual variation in incidence may reflect a seasonal rise and fall in the susceptibility of the host population, timed to the light/dark cycle and mediated by changes in the daily melatonin pulse.
- Predicted consequences: (a) pathogens need not migrate across the equator, (b) nationwide epidemics need not propagate through chains of transmission, (c) hosts are relatively resistant for approximately 6–9 months per year, and (d) the specific physiologic mediator is identifiable and potentially modifiable.
- Susceptibility changes may operate on mucosal surface characteristics, epithelial receptor expression, leukocyte numbers/responsiveness, or other innate/adaptive immune features — distinct for different pathogens.

### Supporting evidence from animal and human physiology

- Many mammalian species (rodents, sheep, deer, monkeys) show well-documented photoperiod-driven reproductive and non-reproductive physiologic cycles; the light/dark cycle (not temperature, humidity, or food) is the controlling signal, replicated by controlling melatonin pulse duration.
- Non-reproductive seasonal physiology documented in mammals: red deer digestive function, mouse seizure threshold, dairy cattle milk composition, Siberian hamster NK-cell activity and lymphocyte blastogenesis under short-day photoperiod, deer-mouse melatonin enhancement of cell-mediated immunity.
- Mice infected with pneumococcus show circadian variation in death rate, modified by lighting conditions (not feeding/activity); susceptibility correlated with cortisone cycle.
- Human evidence: melatonin receptors on lymphocytes (G-protein-coupled); seasonal variation in lymphocyte mitogenic responses, circulating lymphocytes, neutrophils, CD4/CD8 counts, IL-6 in healthy volunteers; seasonal distribution of human conceptions; seasonal affective disorder (SAD) responsive to extended-photoperiod light therapy.

### Direct experimental tests

- Soviet double-blind RCT (Shadrin et al. 1977): febrile reactions to intranasal live-attenuated influenza vaccine were 6.7% in January vs 0.8% in June (p = 0.003, Leningrad, n = 557); four-fold antibody rises were 31–40% in January vs 4.3–4.8% in May/October (p < 0.001, Krasnodar). Winter vaccination produced markedly stronger immunogenic and reactogenic responses.
- Mouse transmission experiments (Schulman & Kilbourne 1963): <1% of mice infected July–October vs 22% in December–January (p < 0.001); replicated with different strain under constant temperature/humidity — 34% infected May–October vs 58% November–April (p < 0.001). Photoperiod conditions not recorded in these experiments.
- Oral polio vaccine seroconversion rates and protection vary by season; vaccine-associated paralytic polio mirrors the seasonal pattern of natural polio.

### Counter-observations acknowledged

- West-to-east progression of rotavirus across the US is not easily explained by host susceptibility changes tied to the light/dark cycle.
- El Niño and warm-weather diarrheal disease hospitalizations in Peru suggest temperature as a direct influence for some pathogens.
- Pandemic influenza emergence argues for chains of transmission rather than simultaneous host-susceptibility waves.

## Limitations

- Perspective paper only: no original data; evidence is synthesised selectively and the epidemiological anomalies chosen as motivating observations could be explained by combinations of existing mechanisms the paper dismisses too quickly.
- The Soviet vaccine trial (Shadrin 1977) is the strongest direct evidence but is a single study, conducted in a limited geographic range, using attenuated strains of uncertain current relevance; it was not replicated per the author's own acknowledgment.
- Mouse transmission experiments (Schulman & Kilbourne 1963) did not record photoperiod conditions; the authors note this gap explicitly. The mechanism linking season to rodent susceptibility was not identified.
- The proposed melatonin mechanism in humans is supported by receptor and correlational data only — no intervention studies in humans directly tested the infectious-disease susceptibility prediction as of 2001.
- The hypothesis does not explain the west-to-east rotavirus wave, pandemic influenza, or warm-weather diarrhoea peaks, suggesting it is incomplete as a universal explanation.
- Published 2001; the mechanistic immunology of seasonal immunity in humans has advanced substantially since (innate immune training, vitamin D seasonality, type I interferon photoperiod regulation), but this paper predates that literature.
- Some immunological seasonal variation cited (lymphocyte counts, mitogenic responses) was of unknown functional significance at the time of writing; subsequent work has been mixed.
