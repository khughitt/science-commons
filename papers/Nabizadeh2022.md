## Key Findings

**Seasonal analysis:**
- Fall: significantly lower relapse risk, RR 0.97 (95% CI 0.95–0.98), Q = 34.12, p = 0.06, I² = 0.02% — very low heterogeneity, robust finding.
- Spring, summer, winter: no statistically significant deviation from the overall average at the seasonal level.

**Monthly analysis:**
- Higher relapse months: March (RR 1.08, 95% CI 1.00–1.16, I² = 74.37%) and April (RR 1.06, 95% CI 1.01–1.11, I² = 40.74%).
- Lower relapse months: August (RR 0.92, 95% CI 0.85–0.98, I² = 65.87%), September (RR 0.97, 95% CI 0.94–0.99, I² = 0.00%), October (RR 0.92, 95% CI 0.89–0.96, I² = 19.68%), November (RR 0.93, 95% CI 0.89–0.97, I² = 24.31%).
- No significant difference detected for January, February, May, June, July, or December.

**Mechanistic candidate drivers discussed:**
1. **Vitamin D and the "seasonal paradox":** Peak sun exposure in midsummer provides a 30–60-day lagged protective effect in late summer/early fall; minimum winter sun triggers spring relapses — consistent with the observed March–April peak and August–November trough.
2. **Melatonin:** Peaks in autumn/winter; independently modulates Th17 and Treg1 cells; no "seasonal paradox" or time lag; supports the fall trough without contradicting the vitamin D lag hypothesis.
3. **Respiratory viral infections:** More common in winter; viral triggers of relapses may manifest 4–8 weeks later in spring (consistent with March–April peak).
4. **Sodium intake:** Seasonal peak in winter; promotes pathogenic Th17 cells via NaCl → Th17 induction.
5. **Air pollution (particulate matter, NOx):** Higher in winter; associated with MS relapses.
6. **Sun exposure independent of vitamin D:** Direct immune-modulatory effects of UV radiation.

Note: no mechanistic data were collected in primary studies; the causal attribution is inferential.

## Limitations

1. **No latitude stratification possible:** The geographic diversity of included studies (Japan to Argentina, Israel to Canada) spans latitudes where seasonal UV/vitamin D amplitude varies greatly. A multinational study (Spelman et al. 2014, cited as ref. 43) showed latitude-dependent seasonal variation, but too few studies allowed subgroup analysis by climate zone or hemisphere. This means the pooled estimates represent a heterogeneous mix.
2. **High heterogeneity in some monthly estimates:** March (I² = 74.37%) and August (I² = 65.87%) show high between-study variance; the pooled RRs for those months should be interpreted with caution.
3. **Relapse definition inconsistency:** No uniform relapse definition across 24 studies; some relied on self-report (recall bias), others on physician-recorded events — a material source of measurement heterogeneity not fully captured by NOS scores.
4. **Missing treatment and MS-type data:** Disease-modifying therapies (DMTs) suppress relapses and may have their own seasonal use patterns; many studies did not report DMT use or MS subtype (RRMS vs. SPMS/PPMS). DMTs that differentially affect immune activation pathways could partially confound the seasonal signal.
5. **Predominantly Northern Hemisphere:** Southern Hemisphere representation is very thin (Brazil, Argentina), limiting generalizability to inverted seasonality contexts.
6. **Cross-sectional seasonal signal:** The meta-analysis pools across calendar-year aggregates from retrospective cohorts; it cannot establish whether individual patients show consistent within-person seasonal rhythm or whether the population-level pattern masks heterogeneous individual cycles.
7. **Publication bias not assessed:** No funnel plot or Egger test was reported.
