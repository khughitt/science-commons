## Key Findings

### Genomic landscape: how similar are DCIS and IDC?

**Synchronous DCIS–IDC pairs** share high genomic concordance: ~83% of the genome overlaps in copy number profiles (Johnson et al.); most frequent shared mutations fall in TP53, PIK3CA, and GATA3. Key differences: IDC shows significantly more mutations and genetic divergence (Fortunato et al.); losses at 5q31.1–5q35.3 and gains at 11p12 are more frequent in DCIS; losses at 6q25.3–6q26 and gains at 13q32.3–13q33.1 more frequent in IDC (Rane et al. meta-analysis). The overall inference is that synchronous DCIS has "fundamentally invasive potential" — most invasive modifications have already occurred at the DCIS stage.

**Primary vs. recurrent DCIS/IDC:** The majority of recurrent cases are clonally related to their primary DCIS (Lips et al., WES + scDNA-seq, n=129 primary + 34 recurrent DCIS + 95 recurrent IDC; 75/95 recurrent IDC clonally related). Wang et al. (Arc-well, n=10 pairs) found an evolutionary bottleneck in most primary DCIS cases: specific chromosome aberrations (chr3q PIK3CA, chr8p MYC/CCNE2, chr20q ZNF217/AURKA) persisted as subclones across primary and recurrent tumors, identified as closely related to DCIS recurrence.

**Pure DCIS vs. DCIS with synchronous IDC:** Pure DCIS generally has fewer CNAs and less genomic instability than DCIS with synchronous IDC (Afghahi et al., n=151; Miron et al., n=31; Bergholtz et al., n=13), with lower frequency of TP53, PIK3CA, and ERBB2 mutations. However, discordant findings exist (Iakovlev et al. reported higher genomic complexity in pure DCIS for specific loci). Conclusion: pure DCIS is likely in a less progressive stage.

### Four models of DCIS-to-IDC progression

1. **Independent lineage model** — DCIS and IDC arise from two distinct normal epithelial cells sharing no overlapping CNAs or mutations; DCIS is a non-obligate precursor. Supported by: IDC-specific gains/losses restricted to IDC component (Johnson et al.); methylation of CDKN2A and CHFR only in DCIS (Moelan et al.); 18%–25% of cases showing no discernible clonal relationship (Lips et al.; Pareja et al.). Undermined by recent large-scale sequencing showing 75%+ clonal relatedness and by Casasent et al.'s topographic scDNA-seq (direct lineage from DCIS confirmed in multiple subpopulations).

2. **Evolutionary bottleneck model** — a specific clone within DCIS is selected and evolves into IDC. Supported by: Arc-well single-cell DNA sequencing (Wang et al. 2023) demonstrating evolutionary bottleneck in 7/10 cases with persistent subclones; general Darwinian framing (Casasent et al.). Implies clonal selection is the operative mechanism at the transition.

3. **Multiclonal invasion model** — multiple subclones escape the basement membrane cooperatively or independently; not a single selected clone but a collective. Two sub-scenarios: mutual cooperation among subclones; "leader–follower" dynamics. Supported by: Casasent et al. topographic scDNA-seq showing co-migration of multiple subclones with shared common ancestor, highly concordant CNAs, and nonsynonymous mutations >87% shared between synchronous DCIS and IDC lesions. The multiclonal model is compatible with clonal ancestry (does not require independent origin) but opposes simple single-clone bottleneck.

4. **Convergent phenotype model** — subclones of different genotypes within DCIS can all give rise to IDC via convergent phenotypic alterations; discordant genomic profiles are compatible with invasion as an epigenetic/phenotypic state transition. Supported by: Yates et al. (two distinct PTEN driver mutations in multifocal DCIS → PTEN-null IDC independently). The model explicitly frames invasion as partly a non-genetic state transition.

**Author conclusion:** The authors argue that clonal selection is the most likely primary mechanism, framing the transition as obeying Darwinian evolution (citing Casasent et al.). They treat all four models as potentially co-existing in different patients or at different phases of progression.

### Role of the TME in progression

The TME is presented as an integral regulator of DCIS progression, not merely a bystander:

- **Myoepithelial cells (MECs):** Loss of MEC integrity is a gating event. Normal MECs express p63, p73, laminin 1, maspin — tumor suppressor functions. DCIS-associated MECs lose p63+TCF7-mediated anti-invasive network; upregulate αvβ6 integrin, activating TGFβ signaling → MMP9/MMP13 → ECM remodeling. DCIS MECs also secrete CXCL12/CXCL14, promoting tumor cell migration. The degree of MEC phenotypic diversity (lower in DCIS than normal breast by MIBI-TOF; accompanied by increased stromal CD4+ T cells and mast cells in IDC) tracks with progression state.

- **Immune cells:** Within DCIS, T cells, B cells, macrophages, and Tregs are elevated relative to normal tissue. IDC shows further increase in all T cell subsets plus B cells, macrophages, and PD-L1+ immune cells. DCIS is enriched for CD4+ helper T cells while IDC favors CD8+ cytotoxic T cells — different immune activation states. CD20hi macrophages (Linde et al., MMTV-HER2 model) promote DCIS progression via NF-κB–driven CCL2 → Wnt-1 secretion → myoepithelial disruption and E-cadherin junction breakdown. High CD163+ macrophage density correlates with worse outcomes and ipsilateral invasive recurrence.

- **Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs):** Upregulate MMP9, MMP14 via NF-κB/COX-2; promote ECM and basement membrane degradation. CAFs in IDC differ markedly from normal-breast fibroblasts. CAF-derived IL-6 promotes progression partly via cathepsin B-mediated ECM degradation. CXCL1/CXCR2 axis from CAFs activates MAPK, NF-κB, Akt, and Stat3 in tumor cells.

- **Spatial heterogeneity:** Stahl et al. (spatial transcriptomics on synchronous DCIS-IDC) found spatial intratumoral heterogeneity among different ductal regions, suggesting different subclones contributed to DCIS progression; DCIS gene expression was divergent between regions.

### Prognostic biomarkers distinguishing progressors from non-progressors

No single biomarker consistently discriminates progressive from non-progressive DCIS. The Oncotype DX DCIS Score (12-gene panel) is the only commercially validated tool; it classifies DCIS into low/intermediate/high recurrence risk but only predicts 10-year recurrence risk after breast-conserving surgery without radiotherapy, limiting applicability. Clinicopathologic factors (nuclear grade, tumor size, age, margin status, comedo necrosis) remain the dominant prognosticators. High Ki67, COX-2 overexpression, HER2 positivity, and ER negativity associate with higher recurrence risk. Molecular data (TP53 mutations, PIK3CA hotspots, chr3q/8p/20q gains) differentiate pure DCIS from synchronous DCIS but have not yet been validated as clinical predictors of progression in prospective cohorts. AI-based spatial analysis of H&E (Klimov et al.) showed promise for predicting 10-year recurrence risk.

## Limitations

- Review article: no new primary data. Conclusions about relative prevalence of the four models rest on aggregating heterogeneous studies with different cohort sizes (6–2,000), tissue types (synchronous vs. recurrent, pure vs. co-occurring), and methods (CGH through scDNA-seq) — comparisons across studies are confounded by these differences.
- The most compelling evolutionary bottleneck data (Arc-well; Wang et al. 2023) come from only 10 paired samples; the authors acknowledge this. Generalizability to the full spectrum of DCIS progression is not established.
- The review does not systematically distinguish whether the "independent lineage" fraction (~18–25% of cases) represents true independent origin, extreme clonal divergence that erases detectable shared markers, or technical sampling artifacts. The distinction matters for estimating how often IDC arises de novo vs. from DCIS.
- Prognostic biomarker sections primarily report associations; no causal or mechanistic links between specific molecular events and the probability of invasion are established with prospective validation.
- All clinical trial data pertain to treatment effects on recurrence rates; none directly measure the mechanistic models (e.g., no trial tests whether interventions that preserve MEC integrity reduce invasion rates).
- TME characterization studies use predominantly immunohistochemistry and bulk transcriptomics; single-cell and spatial resolution of MEC heterogeneity during progression is limited to a small number of recent studies (MIBI-TOF: Risom et al.; spatial transcriptomics: Stahl et al.; Wei et al.).
- The MMTV-PyMT and MMTV-neu GEMM models lack a natural immune system component relevant to human DCIS progression; the MIND model uses immunocompromised hosts. Immune-competent DCIS models are limited.
