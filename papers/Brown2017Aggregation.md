## Key Findings

- **Dilution and group detoxification can make sensitive cells survive without high intrinsic resistance.** When neighboring cells absorb, metabolize, buffer, or detoxify treatment, the resistance burden shifts from the focal cell to the local population.
- **Density can alter treatment response independently of genotype.** The model predicts that high-density tumor regions can be harder to treat even when individual cells would be vulnerable in isolation.
- **Some aggregation effects can help therapy.** "Danger in numbers" and "group sellout" make high-density or highly resistant populations more vulnerable, for example when resistance mechanisms push toxic drug into the shared extracellular space or when dense populations improve immune or drug attack.
- **Spatial and population structure change evolutionary endpoints.** Neighborhood size affects both final tumor density and the resistance strategy selected by treatment.
- **The work reframes resistance as an ecological property.** A clone's effective fitness under therapy is partly a function of local interaction structure, not only drug-target biochemistry.

## Limitations

- The paper is a theoretical model and does not directly validate aggregation effects in patient tumors.
- The aggregation mechanisms are stylized; real tumors may combine several effects with spatial heterogeneity and drug-specific pharmacology.
- The model does not include immune-cell population dynamics, vascular transport, or explicit 3D tumor architecture.
- Translational use requires clinically measurable proxies for local density, perfusion, and cell-cell interaction range.
